Week 11 — Hypothesis Testing Logic

This week in a line: you’ll learn to test whether one specific claimed value is believable, using a test statistic, a p-value, and a clear decide / fail-to-reject rule.

Last week you built a confidence interval — a whole range of plausible values for an unknown population parameter. This week flips the question around. Instead of “what range of values is plausible?” you’ll ask something sharper: “is this one specific claimed value believable, given what my sample shows?” Your roommate insists the coin from the laundry room “seems unfair” — like it lands heads more often than tails. You can’t interrogate a coin, so you flip it, count, and reason from the data. Spelled out formally, that reasoning process is a hypothesis test, and it’s the backbone of almost every “does this actually work?” claim you’ll meet after this course — in a lab report, a business memo, or a news headline.

1Two competing claims: H0H_0H0​ and HAH_AHA​¶

Every hypothesis test starts with two claims about a population parameter that can’t both be true. One is the null hypothesis, H0H_0H0​ — a skeptical, “nothing new going on” claim, always stated as an equality. The other is the alternative hypothesis, HAH_AHA​ — what you suspect might be true instead, stated with <<<, >>>, or ≠\neq=. The logic mirrors a courtroom: you assume H0H_0H0​ (innocence) unless the sample data gives strong enough evidence to abandon it.

If HAH_AHA​ uses ≠\neq=, the test is two-sided (evidence in either direction counts against H0H_0H0​). If HAH_AHA​ uses <<< or >>>, the test is one-sided (only evidence in the stated direction counts).

  • Coin fairness. No reason to suspect a particular direction, so this is two-sided:

    H0:p=0.5HA:p≠0.5H_0: p = 0.5 \qquad H_A: p \neq 0.5H0​:p=0.5HA​:p=0.5

  • Study-skills workshop. The workshop is designed to raise scores, so this is one-sided:

    H0:μ=70HA:μ>70H_0: \mu = 70 \qquad H_A: \mu > 70H0​:μ=70HA​:μ>70

2The test statistic: how far from H0H_0H0​, in SE units¶

Once you assume H0H_0H0​ is true, ask: how surprising is the sample statistic under that assumption? The test statistic answers this the same way Week 6’s z-score did — by measuring distance in standard error units, using the standard errors you built in Weeks 9–10:

Here p^\hat pp^​ and xˉ\bar xxˉ are the sample statistic actually observed; p0p_0p0​ and μ0\mu_0μ0​ are the hypothesized values from H0H_0H0​; and SE0\mathrm{SE}_0SE0​ is the standard error computed as if H0H_0H0​ were true. A large ∣z∣|z|∣z∣ means the sample landed far from what H0H_0H0​ predicted — evidence against it.

3The p-value: probability of data this extreme, if H0H_0H0​ is true¶

The p-value is the probability of getting a test statistic at least as extreme as the one you observed, assuming H0H_0H0​ is true. It is a conditional probability — conditioned on an assumption you haven’t confirmed.

What a p-value is not

A p-value is not “the probability H0H_0H0​ is true.” A p-value of 0.11 does not mean “there’s an 11% chance the coin is fair.” It means: if the coin really were fair, samples this far from 50/50 would turn up about 11% of the time from ordinary sampling variability alone.

The decision rule compares the p-value to a significance level α\alphaα — the risk of a wrong rejection you agree to accept before seeing the data, usually α=0.05\alpha=0.05α=0.05 in this course:

RuleDecisionMeaning
p-value <α<\alpha<αReject H0H_0H0​statistically significant evidence for HAH_AHA​
p-value ≥α\geq\alpha≥αFail to reject H0H_0H0​not enough evidence to abandon H0H_0H0​

Failing to reject H0H_0H0​ is not the same as proving H0H_0H0​ true. It only means the sample didn’t give strong enough evidence to abandon it — absence of evidence isn’t evidence of absence.

Worked Example 1 — Is the laundry-room coin fair? Simulating n=100n=100n=100 flips of a genuinely fair coin (p0=0.5p_0=0.5p0​=0.5, set.seed(1209)) gives 42 heads, so p^=42/100=0.42\hat p = 42/100 = 0.42p^​=42/100=0.42. Under H0H_0H0​,

SE0=0.5(0.5)100=0.05z=0.42−0.50.05=−1.60\mathrm{SE}_0 = \sqrt{\frac{0.5(0.5)}{100}} = 0.05 \qquad z = \frac{0.42-0.5}{0.05} = -1.60SE0​=1000.5(0.5)​​=0.05z=0.050.42−0.5​=−1.60

The sample proportion sits 1.60 SEs below the hypothesized 0.5. For a two-sided test, the p-value adds both tails beyond ∣z∣=1.60|z|=1.60∣z∣=1.60: p≈0.1096p \approx 0.1096p≈0.1096. Since 0.1096≥0.05=α0.1096 \geq 0.05 = \alpha0.1096≥0.05=α, we fail to reject H0H_0H0​: this sample of 100 flips does not give strong evidence the coin is unfair. (It might still be biased — 100 flips just wasn’t enough to tell. More on that below.)

4One-sided tests, and the cost of being wrong¶

Worked Example 2 — Does the study-skills workshop raise scores? CSUB’s math-placement diagnostic (a computer-adaptive exam scored on a 0–200 scale) has historically averaged μ0=70\mu_0=70μ0​=70 points with a known population standard deviation σ=12\sigma=12σ=12 (a stated benchmark built from many prior years, so σ\sigmaσ is treated as known here — the large-sample z story). This term, n=40n=40n=40 students completed a new study-skills workshop before testing; simulating their scores (set.seed(1209), centered 4 points above the historical mean to model a real but modest effect) gives xˉ=75.15\bar x = 75.15xˉ=75.15 and a sample spread s=14.74s=14.74s=14.74. Since σ\sigmaσ is treated as known, the test still uses σ\sigmaσ, not sss, in the standard error (Week 14’s t-test will use sss instead):

SE0=1240=1.897z=75.15−701.897=2.71\mathrm{SE}_0 = \frac{12}{\sqrt{40}} = 1.897 \qquad z = \frac{75.15-70}{1.897} = 2.71SE0​=40​12​=1.897z=1.89775.15−70​=2.71

HA:μ>70H_A:\mu>70HA​:μ>70 is one-sided, so the p-value is only the right tail beyond z=2.71z=2.71z=2.71: p≈0.0033p\approx 0.0033p≈0.0033. Since 0.0033<0.05=α0.0033<0.05=\alpha0.0033<0.05=α, we reject H0H_0H0​: this sample gives strong evidence the workshop group’s average diagnostic score is higher than the historical 70.

Statistical significance vs. practical importance. The average shift (about 5 points) is statistically clear, but the spread among individual students, s≈14.74s\approx14.74s≈14.74, is nearly three times that shift — a large n makes small average effects easy to detect statistically, but a program director still has to ask separately whether a 5-point average bump is worth the workshop’s cost and time across students who vary that much individually.

Type I and Type II errors. No decision rule is ever certain, because you never actually know whether H0H_0H0​ is true. A Type I error is rejecting an H0H_0H0​ that is actually true; by design, this happens with probability exactly α\alphaα. In Example 2, if the workshop truly had no effect, a batch of 40 students would still produce a “significant” result about 5% of the time purely by chance — the price of using α=0.05\alpha=0.05α=0.05. A Type II error is failing to reject an H0H_0H0​ that is actually false. In Example 1, if the laundry-room coin actually is slightly biased, 100 flips may simply not be enough evidence to catch it — we’d fail to reject fairness even though it’s false. Which error costs more depends on context: a factory that stops a good production line (Type I) wastes time and money; a factory that lets a broken line keep running (Type II) ships defective product.

MATH 952 skills refresher — comparing two decimals

Every decision this week comes down to one comparison: is the p-value less than α\alphaα? Line the decimals up on the same number of places and compare digit by digit, left to right. Is 0.1096<0.050.1096<0.050.1096<0.05? Pad α\alphaα to 0.0500: compare the tenths digit first (1 vs. 0) — 0.1096 is bigger, so no, fail to reject. Is 0.0033<0.050.0033<0.050.0033<0.05? Pad to 0.0500: tenths digits tie (0=00=00=0), so compare hundredths (0 vs. 5) — 0.0033 is smaller, so yes, reject. The symbols <<< and >>> always point at the smaller value.

6Key terms¶

  • Null hypothesis, H0H_0H0​ — the skeptical, “nothing new going on” claim about a parameter, always stated as an equality; assumed true unless the data give strong evidence otherwise.

  • Alternative hypothesis, HAH_AHA​ — the claim you suspect might be true instead, stated with <<<, >>>, or ≠\neq=.

  • One-sided / two-sided test — a test where HAH_AHA​ points in one direction (<<< or >>>) vs. either direction (≠\neq=).

  • Test statistic — a standardized measure (here, zzz) of how far the sample statistic is from the value H0H_0H0​ claims, in standard-error units.

  • p-value — the probability of a test statistic at least this extreme, assuming H0H_0H0​ is true.

  • Significance level, α\alphaα — the pre-chosen risk of a wrong rejection; commonly 0.05.

  • Statistically significant — the decision reached when the p-value is less than α\alphaα.

  • Type I error — rejecting a true H0H_0H0​; occurs with probability α\alphaα by design.

  • Type II error — failing to reject a false H0H_0H0​.

Durable skill: critical thinking & quantitative communication

“Did this actually work, or did it just look that way by chance?” is a question that follows you into almost any job — evaluating a new sales script, a software A/B test, a treatment outcome. Hypothesis testing gives you a disciplined, honest way to answer it, and being able to say precisely “the p-value was 0.003, so this result is unlikely to be chance alone” — instead of just “the numbers went up” — is exactly the defensible, evidence-based communication employers and clients expect.
