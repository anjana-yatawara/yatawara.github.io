Week 12 — Review & Exam 2 (Ch 4–5)

This week in a line: no new material — this is your Chapters 4–5 review and worked-example study guide before Exam 2.

Exam 1 asked you to describe what was already sitting in front of you — a batch of numbers, a two-way table, a bell curve you were simply handed. Chapters 4 and 5 ask a harder, more honest question: what can you say about a population you cannot fully see, using only a sample drawn from it? Chapter 4 sharpened the Normal curve from Chapter 3 into an exact instrument — any cutoff, any percentile, no more rounding to the nearest whole standard deviation. Chapter 5 then turned that instrument outward: every sample statistic (p^\hat pp^​, xˉ\bar xxˉ) wobbles a little from sample to sample, and Chapter 5 gave you the vocabulary and formulas — standard error, confidence interval, hypothesis test — to say exactly how much wobble to expect and what to conclude from it. Exam 2 checks whether you can run both halves cold, in whichever order a question demands. This unit is your self-study companion: a review checklist by chapter, the new formula sheet for Ch 4–5, seven fully worked exam-style problems, and a short list of exam-day tips. There is no new content this week — if a term below feels unfamiliar, go back to the week that introduced it before you test.

This week

OpenIntro 4th ed. — cumulative review of §4.1 (Week 8) and §5.1–5.3 (Weeks 9–11). By exam time you should be able to:

  1. Choose between a Normal-model probability, a confidence interval, and a hypothesis test for a given scenario. (Evaluate, §4.1, 5.2–5.3)

  2. Interpret a confidence interval and a p-value correctly in the context of a claim. (Understand, §5.2–5.3)

  3. Explain the conditions behind an inference procedure, and the limits of the technology used to run it, before you trust the result. (Evaluate, §5.1)

1Review checklist: what “ready for Exam 2” looks like¶

Work down this list chapter by chapter. For each bullet, ask yourself: could I do this without any notes or preparation, right now? If not, redo that week’s worked example without looking at the solution first.

1.1Chapter 4 — Working with the Normal Model (Week 8)¶

  • Standardize any value with the z-score z=(x−μ)/σz=(x-\mu)/\sigmaz=(x−μ)/σ and read off the exact proportion below, above, or between two cutoffs using normalcdf (TI) / xpnorm (R) — no more rounding to the nearest whole standard deviation.

  • Work the inverse-Normal direction with invNorm (TI) / xqnorm (R): given a percentile, or a “top ppp%” / “bottom ppp%” statement, find the cutoff value.

  • Translate “at least” (right tail) and “at most” (left tail) into the correct shaded region before you compute anything, and know the -1E99 / 1E99 open-tail convention on the TI.

  • Reconnect the exact method to the empirical rule from Chapter 3: know that “95% within μ±2σ\mu\pm2\sigmaμ±2σ” is a rounded shortcut for the more exact figures normalcdf/xpnorm return at z=±2z=\pm2z=±2 (about 95.4%), and that the empirical rule is a close approximation, not a replacement, for the exact calculation.

  • Judge whether a real distribution is close enough to bell-shaped to trust the Normal model (mean close to median, no long one-sided tail), and name a variable for which it would be a poor choice (e.g., household income).

1.2Chapter 5 — Foundations for Inference (Weeks 9–11)¶

  • Tell a population parameter (μ\muμ, ppp) apart from a sample point estimate (xˉ\bar xxˉ, p^\hat pp^​), and explain that every point estimate carries sampling variability — a different sample would produce a slightly different number.

  • Compute the standard error of a proportion, SEp^=p^(1−p^)/nSE_{\hat p}=\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}SEp^​​=p^​(1−p^​)/n​, and of a mean, SExˉ=s/nSE_{\bar x}=s/\sqrt{n}SExˉ​=s/n​ — the SE is the typical size of that sample-to-sample wobble.

  • State the Central Limit Theorem in plain language: for a large enough sample, the sampling distribution of xˉ\bar xxˉ (or p^\hat pp^​) is approximately Normal, centered at the true parameter, with spread equal to the SE.

  • Build a large-sample confidence interval as point estimate ± z∗⋅SE\pm\, z^*\cdot SE±z∗⋅SE, and interpret it correctly — “we are 95% confident the true [parameter] is between ___ and ___” — never “there is a 95% probability the parameter itself falls in this interval.”

  • Explain how confidence level and sample size each change the margin of error: higher confidence widens the interval; a larger nnn narrows it. Know the three critical values by heart: z∗=1.645z^*=1.645z∗=1.645 (90%), 1.96 (95%), 2.576 (99%).

  • Write a null hypothesis H0H_0H0​ and alternative hypothesis HaH_aHa​ for a one- or two-sided claim, compute a test statistic z=(estimate−null value)/SEz=(\text{estimate}-\text{null value})/SEz=(estimate−null value)/SE, and use the p-value to reach a decide / fail-to-reject conclusion at a stated significance level α\alphaα.

  • Distinguish a Type I error (rejecting a true H0H_0H0​) from a Type II error (failing to reject a false H0H_0H0​), and state the real-world cost of each in a given scenario.

  • Distinguish statistical significance (a small p-value) from practical importance (whether the difference is actually large enough to matter in context).

Exam-style Example 1 (Ch 4) — below, and between, on a known population

A fitness app models the length of songs on its “campus workout” playlist as approximately Normal, X∼N(3.5,0.6)X \sim N(3.5, 0.6)X∼N(3.5,0.6) minutes. To sanity-check that shape, a simulated sample of n=220n=220n=220 songs drawn from exactly this model comes back with mean 3.45 min and median 3.45 min — nearly identical, which is exactly what a genuinely symmetric bell-shaped population should look like.

suppressMessages({library(mosaic); library(BSDA)})
set.seed(1209)

favstats(~songlen_sim)
#>       min       Q1   median       Q3      max     mean     sd   n missing
#>  1.675453 3.074937 3.453437 3.839573 5.437308 3.451742 0.6110 220       0

xpnorm(2.5, mean = 3.5, sd = 0.6)                              # P(X < 2.5): short-track flag
xpnorm(4.5, mean = 3.5, sd = 0.6) - xpnorm(3, mean = 3.5, sd = 0.6)   # P(3<X<4.5): typical window

Exam-style Example 3 (Ch 5) — point estimates, standard error, and why they wobble

Point estimates. In a sample of n=150n=150n=150 CSUB students, 42 report carrying a reusable water bottle to class daily: p^=42/150=0.28\hat p = 42/150 = 0.28p^​=42/150=0.28. In a separate sample of n=45n=45n=45 textbook prices (dollars):

water_phat <- 42/150
se_water <- sqrt(water_phat * (1 - water_phat) / 150)
c(phat = water_phat, SE = se_water)
#>  phat     SE
#>  0.28 0.0367

textbook_price <- c(78,112,95,64,130,88,101,73,119,92,84,145,67,99,108,76,
                     122,90,85,103,71,116,94,82,137,79,106,89,97,68,124,91,
                     100,86,113,74,96,128,80,105,87,118,70,102,93)
favstats(~textbook_price)
#>  min Q1 median  Q3 max     mean      sd  n missing
#>   64 82     94 108 145 96.37778 19.77865 45       0
se_book <- sd(textbook_price) / sqrt(45)
se_book
#> [1] 2.948

p^=0.28\hat p = 0.28p^​=0.28 and xˉ=$96.38\bar x = \$96.38xˉ=$96.38 are both point estimates — single numbers computed from one sample, standing in for the unknown population parameters ppp and μ\muμ. Their standard errors are SEp^=0.28(0.72)/150=0.0367SE_{\hat p} = \sqrt{0.28(0.72)/150} = 0.0367SEp^​​=0.28(0.72)/150​=0.0367 and SExˉ=19.78/45=$2.95SE_{\bar x} = 19.78/\sqrt{45} = \$2.95SExˉ​=19.78/45​=$2.95.

Why “standard error” isn’t just jargon. Imagine (purely for demonstration — real populations are never fully known) a hypothetical coffee-cart wait-time population that truly is N(6,1.5)N(6, 1.5)N(6,1.5) minutes. Draw 1,000 different samples of n=40n=40n=40 from it and compute each sample’s mean:

sim_means <- do(1000) * mean(rnorm(40, mean = 6, sd = 1.5))
favstats(~mean, data = sim_means)
#>       min       Q1   median       Q3      max     mean       sd    n missing
#>  4.93037 5.841774 5.995545 6.152879 6.743429 5.994309 0.241423 1000       0
sd(~mean, data = sim_means)          # 0.241  (simulated SD of the 1000 sample means)
1.5 / sqrt(40)                        # 0.237  (the SE formula)

The 1,000 simulated sample means center right on the true μ=6\mu=6μ=6 and spread out with SD ≈0.241\approx 0.241≈0.241 minutes — almost exactly the formula’s prediction, SE=1.5/40=0.237SE=1.5/\sqrt{40}=0.237SE=1.5/40​=0.237. That match is the entire point of the standard error: it is not a rounding fudge factor, it is a genuine prediction of how far a single sample’s statistic will typically land from the truth.

Exam-style Example 4 (Ch 5) — confidence interval for a proportion

Of n=200n=200n=200 commuting students surveyed, 54 say they carpool at least twice a week. Build a 95% confidence interval for the true campus-wide carpool proportion ppp.

n <- 200; x <- 54
phat <- x / n
se <- sqrt(phat * (1 - phat) / n)
me <- 1.96 * se
c(phat = phat, SE = se, ME = me, lower = phat - me, upper = phat + me)
#>   phat     SE     ME  lower  upper
#>   0.27 0.0314 0.0615 0.2085 0.3315

p^=54/200=0.27\hat p = 54/200 = 0.27p^​=54/200=0.27, SEp^=0.27(0.73)/200=0.0314SE_{\hat p} = \sqrt{0.27(0.73)/200} = 0.0314SEp^​​=0.27(0.73)/200​=0.0314, and with z∗=1.96z^*=1.96z∗=1.96 for 95% confidence, ME=1.96(0.0314)=0.0615ME = 1.96(0.0314) = 0.0615ME=1.96(0.0314)=0.0615. The interval is 0.27±0.0615=(0.2085, 0.3315)0.27 \pm 0.0615 = (0.2085,\ 0.3315)0.27±0.0615=(0.2085, 0.3315).

Read-back: we are 95% confident the true proportion of all commuting students who carpool at least twice a week is between 20.9% and 33.2%. That is not the same as “there’s a 95% chance the true proportion is in this interval” — the true proportion is a fixed (if unknown) number; the 95% describes how often this procedure captures it across many samples.

TI: 1-PropZInt with x=54x=54x=54, n=200n=200n=200, C-Level =.95=.95=.95 matches the hand calculation above exactly, since both use the same point-estimate-±-z∗⋅SEz^*\cdot SEz∗⋅SE formula. R’s prop.test(54, 200) reports a slightly different interval (0.211, 0.338)(0.211,\ 0.338)(0.211, 0.338) because, by default, it uses a more refined (Wilson score, continuity-corrected) formula — close, but not identical, to the Wald formula above. On Exam 2, use the by-hand / TI formula shown here; R is a confirmation tool, and this small mismatch is exactly the kind of “know the technology’s limits” idea worth remembering.

Exam-style Example 5 (Ch 5) — confidence interval for a mean

A large-sample survey of n=120n=120n=120 first-year students finds a mean of xˉ=6.7\bar x = 6.7xˉ=6.7 hours of sleep per night with sample SD s=1.1s = 1.1s=1.1 hours. Build a 90% confidence interval for the true mean sleep μ\muμ.

xbar <- 6.7; s <- 1.1; n <- 120
se <- s / sqrt(n)
me <- 1.645 * se
c(SE = se, ME = me, lower = xbar - me, upper = xbar + me)
#>      SE     ME  lower  upper
#>  0.1004 0.1652 6.5348 6.8652

zsum.test(mean.x = 6.7, sigma.x = 1.1, n.x = 120, conf.level = 0.90)
#> 90 percent confidence interval:
#>  6.534831 6.865169

SExˉ=1.1/120=0.1004SE_{\bar x} = 1.1/\sqrt{120} = 0.1004SExˉ​=1.1/120​=0.1004 hours, and with z∗=1.645z^*=1.645z∗=1.645 for 90% confidence, ME=1.645(0.1004)=0.1652ME = 1.645(0.1004) = 0.1652ME=1.645(0.1004)=0.1652 hours. The interval is 6.7±0.165=(6.535, 6.865)6.7 \pm 0.165 = (6.535,\ 6.865)6.7±0.165=(6.535, 6.865) hours, and zsum.test confirms it exactly (means don’t have the proportion’s Wilson-vs-Wald mismatch).

Read-back: we are 90% confident the true mean nightly sleep of all first-year students is between 6.54 and 6.87 hours — an interval that sits entirely below the commonly recommended 8 hours, worth flagging even though this exam doesn’t ask you to test that claim formally.

Exam-style Example 6 (Ch 5) — hypothesis test for a proportion

A campus tech survey claims 90% of CSUB students own a laptop (p0=0.90p_0 = 0.90p0​=0.90). A random sample of n=180n=180n=180 students finds 152 laptop owners. Test, at α=0.05\alpha = 0.05α=0.05, whether the true proportion differs from the claim.

Step 1 — hypotheses. H0:p=0.90H_0: p = 0.90H0​:p=0.90 vs. Ha:p≠0.90H_a: p \neq 0.90Ha​:p=0.90 (two-sided, since the survey claim is simply challenged, not challenged in one direction).

Step 2 — test statistic, using the null value p0p_0p0​ in the standard error:

n <- 180; x <- 152; p0 <- 0.90
phat <- x / n
se0 <- sqrt(p0 * (1 - p0) / n)
z <- (phat - p0) / se0
p_value <- 2 * pnorm(-abs(z))
c(phat = phat, SE0 = se0, z = z, p_value = p_value)
#>    phat    SE0      z p_value
#>  0.8444 0.0224 -2.485   0.013

prop.test(152, 180, p = 0.90, correct = FALSE)
#> X-squared = 6.1728, df = 1, p-value = 0.01297

p^=152/180=0.8444\hat p = 152/180 = 0.8444p^​=152/180=0.8444, SE0=0.90(0.10)/180=0.0224SE_0 = \sqrt{0.90(0.10)/180} = 0.0224SE0​=0.90(0.10)/180​=0.0224, and z=(0.8444−0.90)/0.0224=−2.485z = (0.8444-0.90)/0.0224 = -2.485z=(0.8444−0.90)/0.0224=−2.485, giving a two-sided p-value ≈0.013\approx 0.013≈0.013 (prop.test’s chi-square version agrees: p=0.01297p=0.01297p=0.01297, matching since its statistic is z2z^2z2).

Step 3 — decision. Since 0.013<α=0.050.013 < \alpha = 0.050.013<α=0.05, we reject H0H_0H0​: this sample gives statistically significant evidence that the true laptop-ownership proportion differs from the claimed 90% — specifically, the sample suggests it’s lower.

Step 4 — errors, in context. A Type I error here would be rejecting the 90% claim when it is actually true (wrongly concluding ownership is off, when the survey’s 90% figure was correct). A Type II error would be failing to reject the 90% claim when the true rate is actually lower (missing a real gap in laptop access that the campus should know about) — arguably the costlier mistake for a department planning tech-equity resources.

TI: 1-PropZTest with p0=.90p_0=.90p0​=.90, x=152x=152x=152, n=180n=180n=180, two-sided, reports the same zzz and p-value.

Exam-style Example 7 (Ch 4/5 synthesis) — which tool do you reach for?

Read each scenario and decide: Normal-model probability (Ch 4), confidence interval (Ch 5), or hypothesis test (Ch 5)? Then solve it.

Figure 1. A three-branch decision-flow diagram matching the exam’s most common setup question: what is the scenario actually asking for?

(a) “A tutoring center’s years of records show appointment lengths follow N(45,8)N(45, 8)N(45,8) minutes. What fraction of appointments run over an hour?” Tool: Normal-model probability — μ\muμ and σ\sigmaσ are already known population values; nothing is being estimated or tested from a sample.

z=60−458=1.875,P(X>60)=1−P(X<60)=0.0304z = \frac{60-45}{8} = 1.875, \qquad P(X>60) = 1 - P(X<60) = 0.0304z=860−45​=1.875,P(X>60)=1−P(X<60)=0.0304

About 3.0% of appointments run over an hour.

(b) “A campus paper wants to estimate the true percentage of all students who favor a proposed fee change, based on a poll of 240 students in which 132 favor it.” Tool: confidence interval — the population proportion is unknown, and the goal is to estimate it from a sample, not test a specific claim about it.

phat <- 132/240; se <- sqrt(phat*(1-phat)/240); me <- 1.96*se
c(phat = phat, lower = phat - me, upper = phat + me)
#>   phat  lower  upper
#>   0.55 0.4871 0.6129

We’re 95% confident 47.9%(lower)–61.3%(upper), more precisely (0.4871,0.6129)(0.4871, 0.6129)(0.4871,0.6129), of all students favor the change — notice the interval straddles 50%, so this poll alone can’t say whether a majority favors it.

(c) “A vending-machine company claims its machines dispense 12 oz per cup on average; a facilities manager samples 50 cups and finds a mean of 11.7 oz with SD 0.9 oz. Are the machines giving customers less than they paid for?” Tool: hypothesis test — there’s a specific numerical claim (μ0=12\mu_0=12μ0​=12) being checked against sample evidence, with a real decision (recalibrate or not) riding on it.

se <- 0.9/sqrt(50); z <- (11.7-12)/se; p_value <- 2*pnorm(-abs(z))
c(SE = se, z = z, p_value = p_value)
#>     SE      z p_value
#> 0.1273 -2.357  0.0184

H0:μ=12H_0:\mu=12H0​:μ=12 vs. Ha:μ≠12H_a:\mu\neq12Ha​:μ=12; z=−2.357z=-2.357z=−2.357, p-value =0.0184<0.05=0.0184 < 0.05=0.0184<0.05, so we reject H0H_0H0​ — statistically significant evidence the machines dispense less than the claimed 12 oz on average.

Read-back: the giveaway phrase in each scenario is the goal: “what fraction/proportion of a known model” → probability; “estimate the true value” → confidence interval; “test a specific claimed number” → hypothesis test. Exam 2 will often make you identify this before you can even start computing.

MATH 952 exam clinic

Three arithmetic habits cause most of the point loss on Exam 2 — none of them are new statistics, just care with numbers:

  • Formula substitution, one step at a time. In z=(x−μ)/σz=(x-\mu)/\sigmaz=(x−μ)/σ, SE=p^(1−p^)/nSE=\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}SE=p^​(1−p^​)/n​, or z=(estimate−null value)/SEz=(\text{estimate}-\text{null value})/SEz=(estimate−null value)/SE: finish the numerator completely (including any subtraction) before you divide or take a square root. Substituting into the wrong piece first is the single most common formula-sheet error.

  • Building the ± interval. A confidence interval is always [point estimate −-− ME, point estimate +++ ME] — compute the point estimate and the margin of error as two separate numbers first, then build the interval as the last step. Don’t try to do it all in one line.

  • Comparing decimals: is the p-value less than α\alphaα? Line the decimals up by place value before comparing — 0.013<0.050.013 < 0.050.013<0.05 is true even though “13” looks bigger than “5” if you compare the digits carelessly instead of the actual decimal values.

Exam-day tips

  • Budget your time roughly by chapter weight: on a 75-minute exam, that’s about 22 minutes on Ch 4 material and 53 minutes on Ch 5 — don’t let one tough Normal-model question eat time you need for the confidence-interval and hypothesis-testing section.

  • Before computing anything, name the tool out loud (to yourself): probability, interval, or test? Example 7 above is exactly the drill to practice this on.

  • Sketch and shade a Normal curve for every Ch 4 question, and write out H0H_0H0​/HaH_aHa​ in symbols for every Ch 5 test question, even when not required — both catch setup errors before you commit to an answer.

  • Show every step. A correctly set up formula earns partial credit even if the final arithmetic slips; a bare final number with no work shown often cannot.

  • Bring your TI-83/84 with fresh batteries and your handwritten, front-and-back formula sheet. No phones, no shared calculators once the exam starts.

  • If you finish early, revisit any confidence-interval or hypothesis-test question and re-read your own interpretation sentence — “we are X% confident that...” and “there is/is not significant evidence that...” are exact phrasings graders look for, and it’s easy to slip into the “probability the parameter is in the interval” error under time pressure.

Durable skill: quantitative reasoning & quantitative communication

Choosing the right tool for a question — probability, interval, or test — before you touch a formula is quantitative reasoning: matching a method to a goal instead of pattern-matching to whichever formula you remember best. Writing the interpretation sentence correctly — “95% confident the true value lies between ___ and ___,” not “95% probability it’s in this interval” — is quantitative communication: saying precisely what the number does and doesn’t claim. Both skills show up the moment you leave this course, whether you’re reading a political poll’s margin of error, a clinical trial’s p-value, or a vendor’s “on average” claim at work.
