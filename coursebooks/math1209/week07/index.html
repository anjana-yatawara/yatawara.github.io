Week 7 — Review & Exam 1 (Ch 1–3)

1Review checklist: what “ready for Exam 1” looks like¶

Work down this list chapter by chapter. For each bullet, ask yourself: could I do this without any notes or preparation, on paper, right now? If not, that’s your next review target — go back to the week named and redo its worked example without looking at the solution first.

1.1Chapter 1 — Data & Study Design (Week 1)¶

  • Classify a variable as numerical (discrete or continuous) or categorical (nominal or ordinal) — and catch the trap where a column of digits (like a birth month) is really categorical because averaging it would mean nothing.

  • Define population, sample, parameter (a fixed, usually unknown number describing the population, Greek letter like μ\muμ), and statistic (a number you compute from a sample, Roman letter like xˉ\bar{x}xˉ) — and correctly label which is which in a description.

  • Identify a sampling method (simple random, stratified, cluster) and name a plausible source of bias in a described sampling plan.

  • Tell an observational study apart from an experiment, and explain in one sentence why only a randomized experiment supports a cause-and-effect claim (the idea of confounding).

1.2Chapter 2 — Summarizing Data (Weeks 2–3)¶

  • Compute the mean, median, mode, sample standard deviation sss, and the five-number summary (min⁡,Q1,median,Q3,max⁡\min, Q_1, \text{median}, Q_3, \maxmin,Q1​,median,Q3​,max) for a list of numbers, by hand or by reading calculator/R output.

  • Compute the IQR (Q3−Q1Q_3 - Q_1Q3​−Q1​) and apply the 1.5·IQR outlier rule to flag unusual values.

  • Read a histogram or boxplot and describe a distribution’s shape (symmetric, left/right-skewed, unimodal/bimodal).

  • Explain why skew pulls the mean away from the median, and why the median and IQR are more resistant to outliers than the mean and SD.

  • Build a frequency / relative-frequency table, and explain why a bar chart’s y-axis must start at 0 to be read honestly.

  • Read a two-way (contingency) table and correctly compute a marginal, joint, or conditional proportion — and know which one a question is actually asking for.

  • Judge from conditional proportions (or a segmented bar chart) whether two categorical variables appear associated.

3Exam-style worked examples¶

Work each one yourself first — cover the solution with your hand or a sheet of paper — then check your steps against what’s shown. Every number below was computed in R (mosaic/BSDA, R 4.5.2) so you can trust it to the decimal place.

Exam-style Example 1 (Ch 1) — Reading a study and its vocabulary

Part A — design. A campus wellness center wants to know whether a 10-minute guided morning walk lowers self-reported stress. They recruit 60 student volunteers and use a random-number generator to assign 30 of them to take the morning walk every day for two weeks and the other 30 to change nothing (control). At the end of two weeks, every volunteer rates their stress on a continuous 0–10 scale.

  • Explanatory variable: whether a student walked or not — categorical, nominal (two labels, “walk” / “no walk,” with no natural order).

  • Response variable: the stress rating — numerical, continuous (it can fall anywhere on the 0–10 scale, and averaging it is meaningful).

  • Observational study or experiment? Experiment — the researchers themselves assigned the treatment (who walks), rather than just observing an existing group of walkers.

  • Why does this design support a cause-and-effect claim? Because the 60 volunteers were randomly assigned to the two groups, the walk and no-walk groups should be similar, on average, in every other way (sleep habits, coursework, personality) before the study starts. If stress ends up lower in the walking group, that difference isn’t explained by some other systematic difference between the groups (a confounding variable) — it’s attributable to the walk itself. An observational study (just asking students whether they already walk in the morning) couldn’t rule out confounding this cleanly, because morning walkers might already differ from non-walkers in ways connected to stress (schedule, motivation, fitness).

Part B — parameter vs. statistic. Separately, to practice the vocabulary: suppose (a luxury real research never actually has) the true mean commute time for all 8,500 CSUB commuter students happens to be known: μ=19.4\mu = 19.4μ=19.4 minutes — this is a parameter. A random sample of n=6n=6n=6 students self-reports commute times, in minutes: 12, 25, 18, 31, 15, 22.

suppressMessages({library(mosaic); library(BSDA)})
commute <- c(12, 25, 18, 31, 15, 22)
favstats(commute)
#>  min    Q1 median    Q3 max mean      sd n missing
#>   12 15.75     20 24.25  31 20.5 6.94982 6       0

The sample mean xˉ=20.5\bar{x} = 20.5xˉ=20.5 minutes is a statistic. Notice xˉ=20.5\bar{x} = 20.5xˉ=20.5 is close to, but not exactly equal to, μ=19.4\mu = 19.4μ=19.4 — a gap of 1.1 minutes. That gap isn’t an error; it’s ordinary sampling variability, the fact that a sample almost never lands exactly on the population value. (We build precise tools for that gap starting in Week 9 — for now, just know the vocabulary that separates a known-in-theory population number from a computed-from-data sample number.)

Exam-style Example 2 (Ch 2, numerical) — mean, median, spread, and an outlier

Eleven classmates share how many songs are on their current music playlist: 18, 22, 19, 25, 21, 20, 23, 19, 24, 17, 52.

songs <- c(18, 22, 19, 25, 21, 20, 23, 19, 24, 17, 52)
favstats(songs)
#>  min Q1 median   Q3 max     mean      sd  n missing
#>   17 19     21 23.5  52 23.63636 9.73933 11       0

Five-number summary: min = 17, Q1=19Q_1 = 19Q1​=19, median = 21, Q3=23.5Q_3 = 23.5Q3​=23.5, max = 52.

Center: the median (21 songs) is a better “typical” value here than the mean (23.6 songs) — the mean is pulled upward by that one long playlist.

Spread: IQR=Q3−Q1=23.5−19=4.5IQR = Q_3 - Q_1 = 23.5 - 19 = 4.5IQR=Q3​−Q1​=23.5−19=4.5 songs.

Outlier check: the fences are

Q1−1.5⋅IQR=19−1.5(4.5)=12.25Q3+1.5⋅IQR=23.5+1.5(4.5)=30.25Q_1 - 1.5 \cdot IQR = 19 - 1.5(4.5) = 12.25 \qquad Q_3 + 1.5 \cdot IQR = 23.5 + 1.5(4.5) = 30.25Q1​−1.5⋅IQR=19−1.5(4.5)=12.25Q3​+1.5⋅IQR=23.5+1.5(4.5)=30.25

The 52-song playlist is well above 30.25, so it’s a formally flagged outlier; every other value falls inside the fences.

Read-back: because of that outlier, the distribution is right-skewed (a long tail toward larger values), which is exactly why the mean (23.6) sits above the median (21) — the classic mean-gets-pulled-toward-the-tail pattern from Week 2.

Exam-style Example 3 (Ch 2, categorical) — a two-way table

A campus dining survey cross-tabulates 180 students’ meal-plan tier (Basic or Premium) with whether they’re satisfied with dining options:

BasicPremiumTotal
Satisfied5472126
Not satisfied361854
Total9090180
dining <- matrix(c(54, 36, 72, 18), nrow = 2,
                  dimnames = list(Outcome = c("Satisfied", "Not satisfied"),
                                   Plan = c("Basic", "Premium")))
prop.table(dining, margin = 2)   # P(Outcome | Plan) -- conditional on each column
#>                Plan
#> Outcome         Basic Premium
#>   Satisfied       0.6     0.8
#>   Not satisfied   0.4     0.2

  • Marginal: overall, 126/180=0.700126/180 = 0.700126/180=0.700, so 70.0% of all 180 students are satisfied, ignoring plan tier entirely.

  • Joint: 54/180=0.30054/180 = 0.30054/180=0.300, so 30.0% of all 180 students are both on the Basic plan and satisfied.

  • Conditional: among Basic-plan students, 54/90=0.60054/90 = 0.60054/90=0.600 (60.0% satisfied); among Premium-plan students, 72/90=0.80072/90 = 0.80072/90=0.800 (80.0% satisfied).

Read-back: the conditional satisfaction rate jumps from 60.0% (Basic) to 80.0% (Premium) — a 20-point gap — so plan tier and satisfaction appear associated. If they weren’t associated, both conditional rates would sit close to the 70.0% marginal rate.

Exam-style Example 4 (Ch 3, probability rules) — complement, addition, multiplication

On a large campus, 62% of students drink coffee regularly (P(C)=0.62P(C) = 0.62P(C)=0.62) and 35% report pulling at least one all-nighter this semester (P(N)=0.35P(N) = 0.35P(N)=0.35). Assume, for this problem, that coffee-drinking and all-nighters are independent events.

pC <- 0.62; pN <- 0.35
p_and <- pC * pN            # multiplication rule (independent)
p_or  <- pC + pN - p_and    # general addition rule
c(p_and = p_and, p_or = p_or, `1-pC` = 1 - pC, `1-p_or` = 1 - p_or)
#>  p_and   p_or   1-pC 1-p_or
#>  0.217  0.753  0.380  0.247

  • Multiplication rule (independence lets you multiply): P(C and N)=0.62×0.35=0.217P(C \text{ and } N) = 0.62 \times 0.35 = 0.217P(C and N)=0.62×0.35=0.217 — 21.7% of students both drink coffee and pulled an all-nighter.

  • Addition rule (general form, since the events can overlap): P(C or N)=0.62+0.35−0.217=0.753P(C \text{ or } N) = 0.62 + 0.35 - 0.217 = 0.753P(C or N)=0.62+0.35−0.217=0.753 — 75.3% of students did at least one of the two.

  • Complement: P(not C)=1−0.62=0.38P(\text{not } C) = 1 - 0.62 = 0.38P(not C)=1−0.62=0.38 (38% don’t drink coffee regularly), and P(neither C nor N)=1−0.753=0.247P(\text{neither } C \text{ nor } N) = 1 - 0.753 = 0.247P(neither C nor N)=1−0.753=0.247 — 24.7% did neither.

Exam-style Example 5 (Ch 3, random variable) — expected value and SD of raffle winnings

A $5 fundraiser raffle ticket pays: $0 with probability 0.90, $10 with probability 0.08, and $100 with probability 0.02.

prize <- c(0, 10, 100)
p     <- c(0.90, 0.08, 0.02)
sum(p)                                  # check the distribution is valid
#> [1] 1
mu <- sum(prize * p)                    # E(prize)
sd_prize <- sqrt(sum((prize - mu)^2 * p))
mu
#> [1] 2.8
sd_prize
#> [1] 14.14779

net <- prize - 5                        # net winnings = prize - $5 ticket cost
sum(net * p)                            # E(net winnings)
#> [1] -2.2
sqrt(sum((net - sum(net * p))^2 * p))   # SD(net winnings)
#> [1] 14.14779

Check the distribution first: 0.90+0.08+0.02=1.000.90 + 0.08 + 0.02 = 1.000.90+0.08+0.02=1.00 — a valid probability distribution.

Expected value: E(prize)=0(0.90)+10(0.08)+100(0.02)=0+0.8+2=$2.80E(\text{prize}) = 0(0.90) + 10(0.08) + 100(0.02) = 0 + 0.8 + 2 = \$2.80E(prize)=0(0.90)+10(0.08)+100(0.02)=0+0.8+2=$2.80. Subtracting the $5 ticket cost, the expected net winnings are E(X)=2.80−5=−$2.20E(X) = 2.80 - 5 = -\$2.20E(X)=2.80−5=−$2.20 — on average, a player loses $2.20 per ticket. That’s normal for a fundraiser: the raffle is designed to have negative expected value for the player, since the difference funds the cause.

Standard deviation: SD(prize)=(0−2.8)2(0.90)+(10−2.8)2(0.08)+(100−2.8)2(0.02)=200.16≈$14.15SD(\text{prize}) = \sqrt{(0-2.8)^2(0.90) + (10-2.8)^2(0.08) + (100-2.8)^2(0.02)} = \sqrt{200.16} \approx \$14.15SD(prize)=(0−2.8)2(0.90)+(10−2.8)2(0.08)+(100−2.8)2(0.02)​=200.16​≈$14.15. Subtracting a constant (the $5 ticket price) shifts the mean but never changes the spread, so SD(net winnings)≈$14.15SD(\text{net winnings}) \approx \$14.15SD(net winnings)≈$14.15 too — confirmed by the R output above. That large SD relative to the $2.20 average loss tells you why people buy raffle tickets despite a negative expected value: the small chance of the $100 prize creates a lot of variability around that average.

Exam-style Example 6 (Ch 3, Normal model) — z-scores and the empirical rule

Exam scores in Section A are approximately Normal with μ=76\mu = 76μ=76, σ=8\sigma = 8σ=8. A Section A student scores 92. Exam scores in Section B (a different exam) are approximately Normal with μ=70\mu = 70μ=70, σ=5\sigma = 5σ=5. A Section B student scores 82. Whose score is more impressive, relative to their own section?

z1 <- (92 - 76) / 8
z2 <- (82 - 70) / 5
c(z1 = z1, z2 = z2)
#>  z1  z2
#> 2.0 2.4

xpnorm(92, mean = 76, sd = 8)   # exact proportion below 92 in Section A
#> If X ~ N(76, 8), then
#>   P(X <= 92) = P(Z <= 2) = 0.9772
#>   P(X >  92) = P(Z >  2) = 0.02275

Standardize both scores: z1=(92−76)/8=2.0z_1 = (92-76)/8 = 2.0z1​=(92−76)/8=2.0 and z2=(82−70)/5=2.4z_2 = (82-70)/5 = 2.4z2​=(82−70)/5=2.4. Even though 92 is a higher raw score than 82, the Section B student is farther above their own class’s mean (2.4 SDs vs. 2.0 SDs) — so the Section B score is the more impressive one, relative to its own distribution. Raw scores from different distributions can’t be compared directly; z-scores put them on the same scale.

Empirical rule, applied to Section A: about 95% of Section A scores fall within μ±2σ=76±16\mu \pm 2\sigma = 76 \pm 16μ±2σ=76±16, i.e., between 60 and 92. That leaves about 5% split between the two tails, so roughly 2.5% of students scored above 92 — the Section A student is around the 97.5th percentile. The exact Normal calculation (which you’ll compute directly starting in Week 8 with xpnorm/normalcdf) gives 97.72%, confirming the empirical rule’s estimate was a close approximation, not a coincidence.

Figure 1. Two bell curves are drawn side by side: Section A’s curve centered at 76 with a shaded right tail starting at 92 (2.0 SDs out), and Section B’s curve centered at 70 with a shaded right tail starting at 82 (2.4 SDs out). Section B’s shaded tail is visibly thinner — farther into its own curve’s tail — even though 82 is a smaller raw number than 92, illustrating why standardizing, not the raw score, decides which result is relatively more impressive.

MATH 952 exam clinic

Three arithmetic habits cause most of the point loss on Exam 1 — none of them are new statistics, just care with numbers:

  • Percents. Converting a count or proportion to a percent is one step: divide, then move the decimal two places right (multiply by 100). 54/90=0.600→60.0%54/90 = 0.600 \rightarrow 60.0\%54/90=0.600→60.0% — don’t stop at the decimal and call it a percent.

  • Order of operations in the SD formula. Square each deviation before you add them up, and take the square root last — squaring after summing gives a wrong, meaningless number.

  • Reading the right cell of a table. Before you divide, ask “a percent (or proportion) of what group?” A conditional proportion divides by a row or column total; a joint proportion divides by the grand total. Mixing these two up is the single most common two-way-table mistake.

Exam-day tips

  • Budget your time roughly by chapter weight: on a 75-minute exam, that’s about 15 minutes on Ch 1 material, 19 minutes on Ch 2, and 41 minutes on Ch 3 — don’t let one tough Chapter 1 question eat time you need for the Chapter 3 section.

  • Read the whole question before you start writing — many points are lost by answering the question you expected instead of the one actually asked (e.g., “conditional” vs. “marginal”).

  • Show every step. Partial credit exists for a correctly set up formula even if the final arithmetic slips — a bare final number with no work shown often can’t earn partial credit.

  • Sketch and shade a Normal curve for every z-score / empirical-rule question, even if it’s not required — it catches sign errors (above vs. below the mean) before you commit to an answer.

  • Bring your TI-83/84 with fresh batteries and your handwritten, front-and-back formula sheet. No phones, no shared calculators once the exam starts.

  • If you finish early, don’t leave — revisit any question where you weren’t fully confident and double-check your rounding and units (minutes vs. hours, percent vs. proportion).

Durable skill: self-directed learning & quantitative communication

Building your own formula sheet and working through a checklist like this one before a big deadline is exactly the skill that transfers past this course: identifying what you actually know versus what you’re assuming you know, then closing that gap yourself before someone else finds it for you. Every workplace has its version of Exam 1 — a client deliverable, a budget review, a certification exam — and the habit of honest self-assessment under a deadline is what you’re practicing this week, not just statistics.
