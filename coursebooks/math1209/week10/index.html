Week 10 — Confidence Intervals

This week in one line: you’ll turn a single wobbly point estimate into a confidence interval — a whole range of plausible values, built as point estimate ± margin of error.

Last week you met the point estimate — a single number like p^\hat pp^​ or xˉ\bar xxˉ, built from one sample, that almost never lands exactly on the true population parameter — and you learned to measure how much that number bounces around from sample to sample with the standard error, SESESE. A single number with no sense of “how far off could this be” is a weak thing to build a decision on.

This week turns that wobbly point estimate into something far more honest: a confidence interval — a whole range of plausible values for the parameter, built around the point estimate, with a stated confidence level attached to how well the procedure itself performs in the long run.

1Point estimate ± margin of error¶

Every confidence interval has the same skeleton:

point estimate ± margin of error (ME)\text{point estimate} \ \pm\ \text{margin of error (ME)}point estimate ± margin of error (ME)

The margin of error is how far, in either direction, the interval reaches from the point estimate. For a large sample, ME=z⋆⋅SEME = z^\star \cdot SEME=z⋆⋅SE, where SESESE is last week’s standard error and z⋆z^\starz⋆ (read “z-star”) is a critical value: a fixed number of standard errors chosen so that the interval-building procedure captures the true parameter a stated percentage of the time. Three critical values come up constantly enough to know by name:

Confidence levelCritical value z⋆z^\starz⋆
90%1.645
95%1.960
99%2.576

A higher confidence level needs a larger z⋆z^\starz⋆: capturing the truth more often requires casting a wider net.

2A confidence interval for a proportion¶

For a sample proportion p^\hat pp^​ (successes xxx out of nnn trials), the large-sample interval is

p^ ± z⋆p^(1−p^)n\hat p \ \pm\ z^\star\sqrt{\dfrac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}p^​ ± z⋆np^​(1−p^​)​​

Worked Example 1 — library hours. CSUB surveys n=400n=400n=400 randomly selected students on extending library hours to midnight during finals week; x=208x=208x=208 say yes, so p^=208/400=0.52\hat p = 208/400 = 0.52p^​=208/400=0.52. The standard error is SE=0.52(0.48)/400≈0.0250SE=\sqrt{0.52(0.48)/400}\approx0.0250SE=0.52(0.48)/400​≈0.0250. For 95% confidence, ME=1.96(0.0250)≈0.049ME = 1.96(0.0250)\approx0.049ME=1.96(0.0250)≈0.049, so

0.52 ± 0.049⟹(0.471, 0.569)0.52 \ \pm\ 0.049 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (0.471,\ 0.569)0.52 ± 0.049⟹(0.471, 0.569)

We are 95% confident the true proportion of all CSUB students who favor extending library hours is between 47.1% and 56.9%.

3A confidence interval for a mean¶

For a sample mean xˉ\bar xxˉ from a large sample, the parallel interval is

xˉ ± z⋆sn\bar x \ \pm\ z^\star\dfrac{s}{\sqrt n}xˉ ± z⋆n​s​

using the sample standard deviation sss as a stand-in for the (usually unknown) σ\sigmaσ — reasonable once nnn is large.

Worked Example 2 — used textbooks. The bookstore’s buy-back records for n=45n=45n=45 used statistics textbooks show a mean resale price of xˉ=$63.40\bar x = \$63.40xˉ=$63.40 with s=$14.75s=\$14.75s=$14.75. The standard error is SE=14.75/45≈$2.20SE = 14.75/\sqrt{45}\approx\$2.20SE=14.75/45​≈$2.20. For 95% confidence, ME=1.96(2.20)≈$4.31ME = 1.96(2.20)\approx\$4.31ME=1.96(2.20)≈$4.31, giving

63.40 ± 4.31⟹($59.09, $67.71)63.40 \ \pm\ 4.31 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (\$59.09,\ \$67.71)63.40 ± 4.31⟹($59.09, $67.71)

We are 95% confident the true mean resale price of a used statistics textbook this semester is between $59.09 and $67.71.

4What “95% confident” really means¶

“95% confident” describes the procedure, not this one interval. Before you collect data, there is a 95% chance that the random sample you’re about to draw will produce an interval that captures the true parameter. Once the data are in hand and the interval is calculated — (0.471,0.569)(0.471,0.569)(0.471,0.569), say — the true proportion either is or is not in that fixed range; there is no randomness left to attach a probability to. The honest phrasing:

“We are 95% confident that the true [parameter] lies between [lower] and [upper]” — meaning: if this sampling procedure were repeated many times, about 95% of the resulting intervals would capture the true parameter.

Two common misinterpretations to avoid.

  • Not correct: “There is a 95% probability the true proportion is between 47.1% and 56.9%.” The parameter is a fixed (if unknown) number, not a random variable — it does not have a “probability” of falling anywhere.

  • Not correct: “95% of students’ true opinions fall in this interval.” The interval estimates one population parameter (the overall proportion), not the spread of individual responses.

About nineteen of the twenty brackets cross the dashed line and about one does not — illustrating that “95%” describes how often the method succeeds across many samples, not the odds attached to any single bracket.

5Confidence level, sample size, and the width of the interval¶

Two knobs control an interval’s width. Raise the confidence level and z⋆z^\starz⋆ grows, widening the interval — a more trustworthy net has to be cast wider. Raise the sample size nnn and SESESE shrinks (it has n\sqrt nn​ in the denominator), narrowing the interval at a fixed confidence level. Using the textbook-price example:

nnnSESESE95% margin of error
45$2.20$4.31
90$1.55$3.05
180$1.10$2.15

Quadrupling nnn (45 → 180) only cuts the margin of error roughly in half, because SESESE shrinks with n\sqrt nn​, not with nnn itself — more data buys precision, but at a diminishing rate.

MATH 952 skills refresher — building a ± interval

“52%±4.9%52\% \pm 4.9\%52%±4.9%” means: start at the center (52%52\%52%), then move that same distance (4.9 points) both left and right. Lower endpoint = center −-− distance =52−4.9=47.1= 52-4.9=47.1=52−4.9=47.1; upper endpoint = center +++ distance =52+4.9=56.9=52+4.9=56.9=52+4.9=56.9. The two endpoints are always the same distance from the center — that is what makes it a ± statement rather than two unrelated numbers.

7Key terms¶

  • Confidence interval — a range of plausible values for a population parameter, built as point estimate ± margin of error.

  • Confidence level — the long-run success rate of the interval-building procedure (e.g., 95% means about 95% of intervals built this way, across repeated samples, capture the true parameter).

  • Margin of error (ME) — the distance the interval reaches in either direction from the point estimate; ME=z⋆⋅SEME = z^\star \cdot SEME=z⋆⋅SE for a large sample.

  • Critical value z⋆z^\starz⋆ — the number of standard errors needed on each side of the point estimate to reach the stated confidence level (1.645 for 90%, 1.960 for 95%, 2.576 for 99%).

Durable skill: quantitative reasoning & critical thinking

News stories, campaign ads, and workplace reports constantly report a number with an implied “give or take.” Knowing that “95% confident” describes a method’s long-run track record — not the odds on one specific interval — is exactly the kind of careful, skeptical reading of a quantitative claim that keeps you from over-trusting, or too quickly dismissing, a poll, a lab result, or a market forecast.
