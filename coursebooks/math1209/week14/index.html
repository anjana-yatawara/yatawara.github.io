Week 14 — Inference for One Mean

This week in a line: you’ll build a confidence interval and run a hypothesis test for a single population mean, using the t-distribution.

It’s a holiday-shortened week, so campus is quieter than usual, but your two biggest new statistics tools of the semester are due right on schedule. Last week you built a confidence interval and ran a hypothesis test for a single proportion — a fraction of people who do something. This week asks the parallel question about an average instead: how long does a typical nap last between classes? Is the average commute to campus really as short as the parking office claims? Both questions need the same two moves you already know — a confidence interval, then a hypothesis test — but for a mean, those moves lean on a new reference distribution, because you almost never know the population’s true spread.

1The t-distribution: the price of not knowing σ\sigmaσ¶

Back in Week 9, the standard error of a sample mean was SExˉ=σ/nSE_{\bar x} = \sigma/\sqrt{n}SExˉ​=σ/n​, where σ\sigmaσ (population standard deviation) was simply given. In real problems, you almost never know σ\sigmaσ — all you have is your sample’s standard deviation, sss. Swapping in sss for σ\sigmaσ seems harmless, but it adds a second layer of guessing on top of sampling variability: sss itself bounces around from sample to sample, especially when nnn is small. To account for that extra wobble, one-mean inference uses the t-distribution instead of the Normal (z) distribution whenever σ\sigmaσ is unknown — which is the normal state of affairs.

The t-distribution is bell-shaped and centered at 0, just like z, but it is flatter in the middle and has heavier tails — more area out past ±2\pm 2±2 or ±3\pm 3±3 — to cover the extra uncertainty from estimating σ\sigmaσ with sss. Exactly how heavy the tails are depends on the degrees of freedom, df=n−1df = n - 1df=n−1: fewer degrees of freedom (smaller samples) means heavier tails and a wider critical value; as nnn grows, dfdfdf grows, and the t-curve tightens until it is nearly indistinguishable from z. Running R’s qt() across a range of dfdfdf makes the convergence concrete:

Notice how t∗t^*t∗ starts well above z∗z^*z∗ at df=5df = 5df=5 and steadily closes the gap — by df=100df = 100df=100 it is only 0.024 away from 1.960, and it would keep sliding closer as dfdfdf grows further. 2A confidence interval for a mean¶

Conditions first. Before building an interval, check: (1) independence — observations come from a random sample or a random process, so one value doesn’t influence another; (2) large sample or nearly normal — with n≥30n \geq 30n≥30 the Central Limit Theorem covers you even if the data are somewhat skewed; with smaller nnn, the sample should show no strong skew and no extreme outliers.

With those conditions met, the confidence interval for a population mean μ\muμ is

xˉ±t∗⋅sn\bar{x} \pm t^* \cdot \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}xˉ±t∗⋅n​s​

where xˉ\bar{x}xˉ is the sample mean, sss is the sample standard deviation, nnn is the sample size, s/ns/\sqrt{n}s/n​ is the standard error of the mean, and t∗t^*t∗ is the critical value cut from the t-distribution with df=n−1df = n-1df=n−1 degrees of freedom at the chosen confidence level.

3The one-sample t-test for a mean¶

A hypothesis test for a mean checks a claim about μ\muμ using the same ingredients. The null hypothesis states a specific value, H0:μ=μ0H_0: \mu = \mu_0H0​:μ=μ0​; the alternative HaH_aHa​ says μ\muμ differs from μ0\mu_0μ0​ (two-sided) or is specifically greater than or less than it (one-sided). The test statistic is

t=xˉ−μ0s/n,df=n−1,t = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}, \qquad df = n - 1,t=s/n​xˉ−μ0​​,df=n−1,

and the p-value is the area under the t-curve with df=n−1df = n-1df=n−1 degrees of freedom, out past the observed ttt in the direction(s) HaH_aHa​ points. As always: small p-value (below your significance level α\alphaα) means the data are surprising under H0H_0H0​, so you reject H0H_0H0​; otherwise you fail to reject H0H_0H0​.

Worked Example 2 — is the campus commute really only 20 minutes?

CSUB’s parking and transportation office advertises an average commute of 20 minutes for commuter students. A random sample of n=45n = 45n=45 commuter students (commute_min_sim) reports xˉ=23.49\bar{x} = 23.49xˉ=23.49 minutes and s=9.39s = 9.39s=9.39 minutes. Many students suspect the true average is longer than advertised, so this calls for a one-sided test.

Hypotheses: H0:μ=20H_0: \mu = 20H0​:μ=20 minutes vs. Ha:μ>20H_a: \mu > 20Ha​:μ>20 minutes.

Conditions. The sample is random, and n=45≥30n = 45 \geq 30n=45≥30, so the large-sample condition covers any mild skew in commute times.

Standard error: SExˉ=9.39/45=1.40SE_{\bar x} = 9.39/\sqrt{45} = 1.40SExˉ​=9.39/45​=1.40 minutes.

Test statistic: df=44df = 44df=44,

t=23.49−201.40=2.49.t = \frac{23.49 - 20}{1.40} = 2.49.t=1.4023.49−20​=2.49.

p-value: the area under the t44t_{44}t44​ curve above t=2.49t = 2.49t=2.49 is p=0.0083p = 0.0083p=0.0083.

Decision: at α=0.05\alpha = 0.05α=0.05, p=0.0083<0.05p = 0.0083 < 0.05p=0.0083<0.05, so we reject H0H_0H0​.

Conclusion in context: the sample gives strong evidence that the true mean commute time for CSUB commuter students exceeds the advertised 20 minutes.

Large samples: why t and z nearly agree. Re-running Example 2’s exact summary statistics through the z-based equivalent (same xˉ\bar xxˉ, sss, and nnn, but referenced against the Normal curve instead of t44t_{44}t44​) gives z=2.49z = 2.49z=2.49 — the identical test statistic value, because the formula is the same — with p=0.0064p = 0.0064p=0.0064, versus the t-test’s p=0.0083p = 0.0083p=0.0083. The two p-values are close enough that both lead to the same decision at α=0.05\alpha = 0.05α=0.05, exactly the pattern the t∗t^*t∗-vs-z∗z^*z∗ table above predicted: once nnn is large (here df=44df = 44df=44), the extra uncertainty from estimating σ\sigmaσ with sss barely matters, and t and z tell the same story. For small samples, they can disagree more, which is why this course leads with t.

MATH 952 skills refresher — dividing by a square root, and reading a t-table row

Standard error is a two-step calculation. SExˉ=s/nSE_{\bar x} = s/\sqrt{n}SExˉ​=s/n​ means: (1) take the square root of nnn first, (2) then divide sss by that result — never divide first. For Example 1: 24≈4.899\sqrt{24} \approx 4.89924​≈4.899, then 12.24/4.899≈2.5012.24 / 4.899 \approx 2.5012.24/4.899≈2.50. Skipping straight to “12.24/2412.24/2412.24/24” gives a wrong, much smaller number.

Reading a t-table. A printed t-table has one row per dfdfdf and one column per confidence level (or per tail area). Find your row (df=n−1df = n-1df=n−1), then the column for your confidence level, and read off t∗t^*t∗ — no interpolation needed for the values used in this course. The row for df=23df = 23df=23 (Example 1) reads:

231.7142.0692.807

Notice t∗t^*t∗ grows as confidence grows (a wider interval to be more sure) — the same pattern you saw with z∗z^*z∗ back in Week 10.

5Key terms¶

  • Standard error of the mean (SExˉSE_{\bar x}SExˉ​) — s/ns/\sqrt{n}s/n​; how much sample means typically vary from the true population mean.

  • t-distribution — a bell-shaped distribution like the Normal curve, but with heavier tails, used for mean inference when the population SD σ\sigmaσ is unknown (estimated by sss).

  • Degrees of freedom (dfdfdf) — for one-mean inference, df=n−1df = n - 1df=n−1; controls how heavy the t-distribution’s tails are.

  • Critical value (t∗t^*t∗) — the t-value cutting off the confidence level’s tail area(s); larger than the matching z∗z^*z∗ for small dfdfdf, converging to it as dfdfdf grows.

  • Confidence interval for a mean — xˉ±t∗⋅s/n\bar{x} \pm t^* \cdot s/\sqrt{n}xˉ±t∗⋅s/n​.

  • One-sample t-test — tests H0:μ=μ0H_0: \mu = \mu_0H0​:μ=μ0​ using t=(xˉ−μ0)/(s/n)t = (\bar{x} - \mu_0)/(s/\sqrt{n})t=(xˉ−μ0​)/(s/n​) with df=n−1df = n-1df=n−1.

  • TInterval / T-Test — the TI-83/84 menu items (under STAT ▸ TESTS) for a one-mean confidence interval and hypothesis test.

  • t.test() / tsum.test() / zsum.test() — the matching R functions: t.test() from raw data (mosaic), tsum.test() from summary statistics, and zsum.test() as the large-sample z equivalent when σ\sigmaσ is treated as known (BSDA).

Durable skill: quantitative reasoning & quantitative communication

Reporting “the average commute exceeds 20 minutes, p=0.0083p = 0.0083p=0.0083” only helps a decision-maker if you can also say what that means in plain language and what evidence backs it up. Employers in every field — operations, healthcare, nonprofits, city planning — need people who can run the right test and explain the conclusion to someone who has never heard of a p-value.
