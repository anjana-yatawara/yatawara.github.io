Week 8 — Working with the Normal Model

This week in one line: you’ll turn any z-score into an exact probability (or work backward from a probability to a value) using a z-table, a calculator, or R — no more “somewhere between.”

Last week you read a bell curve with the 68–95–99.7 rule, but that rule only answers questions about whole standard deviations — “within 1 SD,” “within 2 SD.” Real questions are almost never that tidy. A vet wants to know what fraction of adult cats weigh under a certain number of pounds. A commuter wants to know how likely a 30-minute drive is before an 8 a.m. class. A professor needs the exact score that separates the top 15% of an exam from everyone else — the same kind of cutoff a clothing brand needs when it sizes a run of campus 5K t-shirts.

None of those cutoffs land on a clean whole-SD line, so this week you trade the rough guideline for the exact tool: standardize any value into a z-score, then read off a probability (or work backward from a probability to a value) using a z-table, a calculator, or R. Same bell curve — just precise enough to answer any question you actually get asked.

1Standardizing any value: the z-score formula¶

A z-score rescales a value xxx from a Normal model N(μ,σ)N(\mu, \sigma)N(μ,σ) into “how many standard deviations above or below the mean” that value sits:

z=x−μσz = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}z=σx−μ​

Here xxx is the value you care about, μ\muμ is the population mean, σ\sigmaσ is the population standard deviation, and zzz is the standardized score — negative when xxx is below the mean, positive when it’s above, and unitless (pounds or minutes or points cancel out) so it always compares to the same standard Normal model, N(0,1)N(0,1)N(0,1).

Once you have zzz, three equivalent tools turn it into a probability: a printed z-table (cumulative area to the left of zzz), a calculator sequence, or an R function — all three read the identical curve, so they always agree (up to rounding). A z-table is organized by row (the ones-and-tenths digit of zzz) and column (the hundredths digit); the cell where they cross is P(Z<z)P(Z < z)P(Z<z). Here is the row for z=−1.60z = -1.60z=−1.60 through -1.69:

zzz.00.01.02.03.04.05.06.07.08.09
−1.60.05480.05370.05260.05160.05050.04950.04850.04750.04650.0455

To look up z=−1.67z = -1.67z=−1.67: find row -1.6, column .07, and read P(Z<−1.67)≈0.0475P(Z < -1.67) \approx 0.0475P(Z<−1.67)≈0.0475. A table only stores zzz rounded to two decimals, so it’s a close approximation to the exact value a calculator or R computes from the un-rounded zzz — you’ll see both below.

2Below, above, and between: three proportion questions¶

Worked Example 1 — Cat weights. Model the weight of an adult indoor house cat as X∼N(10,1.8)X \sim N(10, 1.8)X∼N(10,1.8) pounds. To sanity-check that shape, simulate n=200n = 200n=200 cats from exactly that model (cat_weights_sim, set.seed(1209)): the sample comes back with mean 9.89 lb and median 9.85 lb — nearly identical — spread fairly evenly from a minimum of 4.53 to a maximum of 15.81 lb. A mean that close to the median, with no long stretch of extreme values on one side, is exactly what you’d expect from a genuinely symmetric, bell-shaped population, so the Normal model is a reasonable fit here. (It should look that way — the sample was built from a Normal model. For real data you’d check the same two things: does the mean sit close to the median, and does a histogram look roughly symmetric and single-peaked with no long one-sided tail? A variable like household income, which has a long right tail of high earners, fails that check badly and the Normal model would be a poor, misleading choice for it.)

(a) Below a cutoff. A vet flags an adult cat under 7 lb for a closer look. Standardize: z=(7−10)/1.8=−1.667z = (7-10)/1.8 = -1.667z=(7−10)/1.8=−1.667. Here’s the bridge from that z-score to a probability: rounding to z≈−1.67z \approx -1.67z≈−1.67 and reading the z-table row shown above (row -1.6, column .07) gives P(X<7)≈0.0475P(X < 7) \approx 0.0475P(X<7)≈0.0475; R’s xpnorm (below), which uses the un-rounded zzz, gives the more precise P(X<7)=0.0478P(X < 7) = 0.0478P(X<7)=0.0478 — about 4.8% of adult cats are predicted to fall below 7 lb. Every other part of this example uses that same two-step bridge (round zzz to two decimals, look it up — by table, calculator, or R) even when it isn’t spelled out again.

(b) Between two cutoffs. A “healthy range” is 8 to 12 lb. Standardizing both ends gives z8=−1.111z_8 = -1.111z8​=−1.111 and z12=1.111z_{12} = 1.111z12​=1.111. Looking up z12=1.11z_{12}=1.11z12​=1.11 in a z-table (or letting R’s xpnorm compute it exactly below) gives P(X<12)=0.8667P(X<12)=0.8667P(X<12)=0.8667; the same lookup at z8=−1.11z_8=-1.11z8​=−1.11 gives P(X<8)=0.1333P(X<8)=0.1333P(X<8)=0.1333. Subtracting the two areas leaves just the region between them: P(8<X<12)=P(X<12)−P(X<8)=0.8667−0.1333=0.7335P(8 < X < 12) = P(X<12) - P(X<8) = 0.8667 - 0.1333 = 0.7335P(8<X<12)=P(X<12)−P(X<8)=0.8667−0.1333=0.7335 — about 73.3% of adult cats fall in the healthy range.

(c) Above a cutoff. An “overweight” flag is 13 lb or more. Since z13=1.667z_{13} = 1.667z13​=1.667, P(X≥13)=1−P(X<13)=1−0.9522=0.0478P(X \ge 13) = 1 - P(X<13) = 1 - 0.9522 = 0.0478P(X≥13)=1−P(X<13)=1−0.9522=0.0478 — about 4.8%. Notice this exactly matches part (a): 7 and 13 lb are each 3 lb (1.667 SD) from the mean of 10, and a Normal curve is symmetric, so its two matching tails always hold equal area.

Worked Example 2 — Commute times. Model the drive time to an 8 a.m. class as X∼N(22,6)X \sim N(22, 6)X∼N(22,6) minutes (commute_times_sim, n=500n=500n=500, set.seed(1209); sample mean 22.34, sd 5.87 — consistent with the model). “At least 30 minutes” means the value 30 and everything above it, i.e. the right tail: z=(30−22)/6=1.333z = (30-22)/6 = 1.333z=(30−22)/6=1.333, so P(X≥30)=1−P(X<30)=1−0.9088=0.0912P(X \ge 30) = 1 - P(X<30) = 1 - 0.9088 = 0.0912P(X≥30)=1−P(X<30)=1−0.9088=0.0912 — about 9.1% of commutes run 30 minutes or longer. A “typical” commute, between 15 and 25 minutes, uses z15=−1.167z_{15}=-1.167z15​=−1.167 and z25=0.5z_{25}=0.5z25​=0.5: P(15<X<25)=0.6915−0.1217=0.5698P(15<X<25) = 0.6915 - 0.1217 = 0.5698P(15<X<25)=0.6915−0.1217=0.5698 — about 57.0% of commutes fall in that typical window.

3Working backward: the inverse Normal for a percentile¶

Sometimes you’re given the probability and need the cutoff — the reverse of Examples 1–2.

Worked Example 3 — Exam scores. Model an exam score as X∼N(72,9)X \sim N(72, 9)X∼N(72,9) points (exam_scores_sim, n=150n=150n=150, set.seed(1209); sample mean 70.89, sd 9.01 — consistent with the model). The instructor wants the cutoff for a highest-honors citation given to the top 15% of the class. “Top 15%” means the upper 15% of the curve, so it starts at the 100−15=85100-15=85100−15=85th percentile: solving for the score with 85%85\%85% of the curve below it gives x=81.33x = 81.33x=81.33 — a score of about 81.3 or higher places a student in the top 15%. The instructor also wants a cutoff to flag the bottom 10% for extra support: that’s the 10th percentile directly, x=60.47x = 60.47x=60.47 — a score of about 60.5 or lower falls in the bottom 10%.

On your TI-83/84

Both functions live under 2ND VARS (DISTR).

  • normalcdf(lower, upper, μ, σ) returns the area (probability) between two cutoffs — this answers “below,” “above,” and “between” questions. For an open-ended tail, use -1E99 (the EE key, then 99, gives scientific-notation “negative infinity”) as the lower bound for “at most,” or 1E99 as the upper bound for “at least.”

    • Ex. 1(a): normalcdf(-1E99, 7, 10, 1.8) → 0.0478

    • Ex. 1(b): normalcdf(8, 12, 10, 1.8) → 0.7335

    • Ex. 1(c): normalcdf(13, 1E99, 10, 1.8) → 0.0478

    • Ex. 2: normalcdf(30, 1E99, 22, 6) → 0.0912; normalcdf(15, 25, 22, 6) → 0.5698

  • invNorm(area, μ, σ) goes the other direction: give it the area to the left, get back the value. For a “top ppp%” cutoff, use invNorm(1 - p, μ, σ).

    • Ex. 3: invNorm(0.85, 72, 9) → 81.33 (top 15%); invNorm(0.10, 72, 9) → 60.47 (bottom 10%)

  • ShadeNorm(lower, upper, μ, σ), found under 2ND VARS ▸ DRAW, draws the curve with the matching region shaded — use it to picture any of the calls above before you trust the number.

MATH 952 skills refresher — substituting carefully, and “at least” vs. “at most”

Order of operations in z=(x−μ)/σz=(x-\mu)/\sigmaz=(x−μ)/σ: the subtraction happens first, then you divide the whole result by σ\sigmaσ — treat the numerator as if it were in parentheses. For x=7x=7x=7, μ=10\mu=10μ=10, σ=1.8\sigma=1.8σ=1.8: compute 7−10=−37-10=-37−10=−3 first, then divide, −3÷1.8=−1.667-3 \div 1.8 = -1.667−3÷1.8=−1.667. Dividing before subtracting (e.g., 7÷1.87 \div 1.87÷1.8 first) gives a meaningless number.

“At least” vs. “at most”: “at least” means “this value or anything bigger” — the right tail, so you either use normalcdf(value, 1E99, μ, σ) or take the complement 1−P(X<value)1 - P(X<\text{value})1−P(X<value). “At most” means “this value or anything smaller” — the left tail, read directly as normalcdf(-1E99, value, μ, σ), no complement needed. When in doubt, sketch the curve and shade the side the phrase describes before you compute anything.

5Key terms¶

  • z-score — z=(x−μ)/σz=(x-\mu)/\sigmaz=(x−μ)/σ; how many standard deviations a value sits above (positive) or below (negative) the mean of its Normal model.

  • Standard Normal model, N(0,1)N(0,1)N(0,1) — the Normal curve with mean 0 and SD 1 that every z-score is measured against.

  • z-table — a printed table of cumulative areas P(Z<z)P(Z<z)P(Z<z), organized by row (ones-and-tenths of zzz) and column (hundredths of zzz); stores zzz rounded to two decimals.

  • normalcdf(lower, upper, μ, σ) — TI-83/84 function returning the area (probability) between two cutoffs on a Normal curve; matches xpnorm(q, mean=, sd=) in R.

  • invNorm(area, μ, σ) — TI-83/84 function returning the value with a given area to its left (the inverse-Normal/percentile problem); matches xqnorm(p, mean=, sd=) in R.

  • ShadeNorm(lower, upper, μ, σ) — TI-83/84 draw command that shades the requested region on a Normal curve.

  • Percentile — the value below which a stated percentage of the distribution falls (e.g., the 85th percentile has 85% of values below it).

  • Cutoff / threshold — the specific data value marking a probability boundary (e.g., “the score for the top 15%”).

  • Approximately Normal — a distribution close enough to bell-shaped and symmetric (mean near median, no long one-sided tail) that the Normal model gives trustworthy probabilities.

Durable skill: quantitative reasoning & critical thinking

Setting a data-driven cutoff — an underweight threshold, a late-commute risk, an honors-score line, a manufacturing tolerance, a credit-risk score — is a Normal-model calculation in almost every field. Knowing how to translate a plain-English phrase like “at least” or “the top 15%” into the right shaded region, and knowing when a bell curve is not the right model to trust, are skills that transfer directly to any job that sets thresholds from data.
