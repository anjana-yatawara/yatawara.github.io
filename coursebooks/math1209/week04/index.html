Quiz 2 (Ch 2) is given in class this week — it covers Week 2 (summarizing numerical data).

The student government’s fall fundraiser sets up a table outside the union: a $2 raffle ticket next to it, and the campus coffee cart’s “lucky card” promotion — draw one card from a shuffled deck, win a free drink if it’s a heart. Both games raise the same question a smart customer should ask before playing: what are my actual chances, and what should I expect to walk away with? Last week you learned to read probability as long-run relative frequency and met the complement rule. This week adds two more building-block rules — for combining probabilities with “or” and with “and” — and introduces a new tool, the random variable, that finally lets you answer the second question: not just “will I win?” but “what’s this worth, on average?”

1Combining events with “or”: the addition rule¶

Every probability lives on the probability scale: a number from 0 (the event never happens) to 1 (it always happens) — you’ll see this written as a decimal or a percent. Recall from last week that the complement of an event AAA, written AcA^cAc, is “AAA does not happen,” and P(Ac)=1−P(A)P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)P(Ac)=1−P(A). This week’s first new idea is combining two events with the word “or.” The union of events AAA and BBB — written A or BA \text{ or } BA or B — is the event that at least one of them happens.

If AAA and BBB can never happen on the same outcome, they’re called disjoint (or mutually exclusive), and P(A and B)=0P(A \text{ and } B) = 0P(A and B)=0. For disjoint events, probabilities simply add:

P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)(disjoint events)P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) \qquad \text{(disjoint events)}P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)(disjoint events)

If AAA and BBB can happen together, adding P(A)+P(B)P(A)+P(B)P(A)+P(B) double-counts the outcomes where both occur, so the general addition rule subtracts that overlap once:

P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A and B)P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A and B)

Here P(A and B)P(A \text{ and } B)P(A and B) is the joint probability that both events happen at once.

Worked Example 1 — the coffee cart’s lucky-card game. The cart uses a standard, well-shuffled 52-card deck; a customer draws one card, and every card is equally likely (1/521/521/52 each). Let AAA = “the card is a heart” and BBB = “the card is a face card” (jack, queen, or king, in any suit). There are 13 hearts, so P(A)=13/52=0.25P(A) = 13/52 = 0.25P(A)=13/52=0.25. There are 12 face cards, so P(B)=12/52≈0.2308P(B) = 12/52 \approx 0.2308P(B)=12/52≈0.2308.

Complement. The probability of not drawing a heart: P(Ac)=1−0.25=0.75P(A^c) = 1 - 0.25 = 0.75P(Ac)=1−0.25=0.75 (75%).

Disjoint case. “Heart or spade” — a single card can’t be both suits at once, so these events are disjoint, and P(A and spade)=0P(A \text{ and spade}) = 0P(A and spade)=0:

P(heart or spade)=1352+1352=2652=0.50P(\text{heart or spade}) = \tfrac{13}{52} + \tfrac{13}{52} = \tfrac{26}{52} = 0.50P(heart or spade)=5213​+5213​=5226​=0.50

General case. “Heart or face card” is different — the jack, queen, and king of hearts belong to both events, so P(A and B)=3/52≈0.0577P(A \text{ and } B) = 3/52 \approx 0.0577P(A and B)=3/52≈0.0577. Simply adding P(A)+P(B)P(A)+P(B)P(A)+P(B) would count those three cards twice, so the general rule subtracts the overlap:

P(A or B)=1352+1252−352=2252≈0.4231P(A \text{ or } B) = \tfrac{13}{52} + \tfrac{12}{52} - \tfrac{3}{52} = \tfrac{22}{52} \approx 0.4231P(A or B)=5213​+5212​−523​=5222​≈0.4231

About 42.3% of draws win at least one prize.

Independence and the multiplication rule. Two events are independent if knowing the outcome of one tells you nothing about the other — like two different customers each drawing from their own separately shuffled deck. For independent events, the probability both happen is the product:

P(A and B)=P(A)×P(B)(independent events)P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B) \qquad \text{(independent events)}P(A and B)=P(A)×P(B)(independent events)

So the probability that two independent customers both draw hearts is 0.25×0.25=0.06250.25 \times 0.25 = 0.06250.25×0.25=0.0625 (6.25%).

The same rule governs any two independent processes, even something as ordinary as two coin flips. Simulating 1000 pairs of independent fair-coin flips (set.seed(1209)) landed both heads 261 times out of 1000 — an empirical probability of 261/1000=0.261261/1000 = 0.261261/1000=0.261 — close to the theoretical 0.5×0.5=0.250.5 \times 0.5 = 0.250.5×0.5=0.25 the multiplication rule predicts; the small gap is exactly the random noise you’d expect from only 1000 repetitions.

Figure 1. A bar chart of 1000 simulated two-coin-flip repetitions shows three bars — 0 heads, 1 head, 2 heads — at heights 243, 496, and 261 out of 1000. The middle bar (exactly one head) is roughly twice as tall as each outer bar, matching the lopsided-in-the-middle shape the multiplication rule predicts for two independent coins.

2Random variables and probability distributions¶

A random variable, usually written XXX, assigns a number to the outcome of a random process — dollars won, number of heads, minutes late. A discrete random variable takes on a countable list of specific values (a continuous random variable, coming in two weeks, can take any value in a range). The probability distribution of XXX is a table listing every possible value xxx alongside P(x)P(x)P(x), the probability XXX equals that value. Because the table lists every possibility and no two rows overlap, a valid distribution always satisfies ∑P(x)=1\sum P(x) = 1∑P(x)=1.

Worked Example 2 — the fundraiser raffle. The raffle sells n=500n=500n=500 tickets at $2 each. Prizes: 1 grand prize of $250, 4 prizes of $50, and 20 prizes of $10; the remaining tickets win nothing. Let XXX = the dollar amount a randomly selected ticket wins:

xxx (dollars won)P(x)P(x)P(x)
2501/500 = 0.002
504/500 = 0.008
1020/500 = 0.040
0475/500 = 0.950
Total1.000

The probabilities sum to exactly 1, confirming this is a valid distribution.

Expected value. The expected value of a discrete random variable is the probability-weighted average of its possible values:

E(X)=∑x⋅P(x)E(X) = \sum x \cdot P(x)E(X)=∑x⋅P(x)

Here ∑\sum∑ means “add up every row,” xxx is each possible value, and P(x)P(x)P(x) is its probability. E(X)E(X)E(X) (also written μ\muμ, “mu”) is the long-run average — not what any single ticket wins, but what the average payout per ticket would settle toward if the same raffle ran over and over. Multiplying and adding row by row:

E(X)=250(0.002)+50(0.008)+10(0.040)+0(0.950)=0.50+0.40+0.40+0.00=1.30E(X) = 250(0.002) + 50(0.008) + 10(0.040) + 0(0.950) = 0.50 + 0.40 + 0.40 + 0.00 = 1.30E(X)=250(0.002)+50(0.008)+10(0.040)+0(0.950)=0.50+0.40+0.40+0.00=1.30

So E(X)=$1.30E(X) = \$1.30E(X)=$1.30. Since a ticket costs $2, the expected net result of playing is 1.30−2.00=−$0.701.30 - 2.00 = -\$0.701.30−2.00=−$0.70 per ticket — on average, a player loses about 70 cents per ticket, which is exactly the money that funds the student-government fundraiser. No individual ticket ever actually wins $1.30; the number describes the average over many, many tickets.

MATH 952 skills refresher — multiplying decimals and fractions (“of” means multiply)

Every term in an expected-value sum is a multiplication, and the word “of” is your signal to multiply. “0.008 of the $50 prizes” literally means 0.008×500.008 \times 500.008×50. To multiply decimals by hand: multiply the digits as whole numbers, then place the decimal point by counting total decimal places in both factors. For 250×0.002250 \times 0.002250×0.002: multiply 250×2=500250 \times 2 = 500250×2=500, then place the decimal 3 places from the right (since 0.002 has 3 decimal places) to get 0.500. Likewise 50×0.008=0.40050 \times 0.008 = 0.40050×0.008=0.400 and 10×0.040=0.40010 \times 0.040 = 0.40010×0.040=0.400. Once every term is a decimal, add them the same way you’d add money — line up the decimal points: 0.500+0.400+0.400+0.000=1.3000.500 + 0.400 + 0.400 + 0.000 = 1.3000.500+0.400+0.400+0.000=1.300.

4Key terms¶

  • Probability scale — probability is always a number from 0 (never happens) to 1 (always happens).

  • Complement (AcA^cAc) — the event “AAA does not happen”; P(Ac)=1−P(A)P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)P(Ac)=1−P(A).

  • Union (“or”) — the event that at least one of AAA or BBB happens.

  • Disjoint (mutually exclusive) events — events that cannot both happen on the same outcome, so P(A and B)=0P(A \text{ and } B) = 0P(A and B)=0.

  • Addition rule (disjoint) — P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B)P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B), valid only when AAA and BBB are disjoint.

  • Addition rule (general) — P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A and B)P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A and B); the subtraction removes double-counted outcomes.

  • Joint probability, P(A and B)P(A \text{ and } B)P(A and B) — the probability that both AAA and BBB happen.

  • Independent events — knowing whether one event happened gives no information about the other.

  • Multiplication rule (independent events) — P(A and B)=P(A)×P(B)P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B)P(A and B)=P(A)×P(B).

  • Random variable (XXX) — a variable that assigns a number to the outcome of a random process.

  • Discrete probability distribution — a table of every possible value xxx of a discrete random variable with its probability P(x)P(x)P(x); a valid table always has ∑P(x)=1\sum P(x) = 1∑P(x)=1.

  • Expected value (E(X)E(X)E(X), or μ\muμ) — ∑x⋅P(x)\sum x \cdot P(x)∑x⋅P(x); the probability-weighted average value of XXX, interpreted as its long-run average over many repetitions.

Durable skill: quantitative reasoning & critical thinking

Expected value is exactly how businesses and organizers decide whether a game, promotion, or risk is worth it — an insurer pricing a policy, a marketer running a raffle, a manager weighing a costly gamble against a safe bet all boil the decision down to one weighted-average number. Being able to build the distribution table and compute E(X)E(X)E(X) yourself means you can check anyone’s claim about “expected” outcomes instead of taking their word for it.
