Week 13 — Inference for One Proportion

This week in a line: you’ll build a confidence interval and run a hypothesis test for a single population proportion.

A shelter’s adoption newsletter says “86% of our adoptions are forever homes.” A flyer outside the Student Union claims “90% of CSUB students commute rather than live on campus.” Both are claims about a population proportion — the share of an entire group that has some trait — and neither claim was checked by asking every pet owner or every student. Someone sampled a few hundred cases and reported a percentage. The last two weeks gave you the general logic of turning a sample statistic into a confidence interval and a hypothesis test: point estimate, standard error, critical value or p-value, decision. This week reuses every piece of that machinery on the single most common number in daily life — a proportion — and adds exactly one new wrinkle: a condition that tells you when the Normal-curve shortcut is trustworthy at all.

1The sample proportion and the success–failure condition¶

Let ppp (the population proportion) be the true, usually unknown, share of a population that has some trait. You estimate it with the sample proportion,

p^=xn\hat p = \frac{x}{n}p^​=nx​

where xxx is the number of “successes” (cases with the trait) in a random sample of size nnn. “Success” is just labeling language borrowed from coin flips — it means “the outcome you’re counting,” not “a good outcome.” p^\hat pp^​ is a statistic; ppp is the parameter it estimates, exactly the population/sample distinction from Week 1.

Every method this week leans on one fact: when a sample is big enough, the sampling distribution of p^\hat pp^​ is close to Normal, centered at ppp, with standard error SE=p(1−p)/nSE = \sqrt{p(1-p)/n}SE=p(1−p)/n​ — the Week 9 idea, applied to a proportion. “Big enough” has a name, the success–failure condition: you need at least 10 expected successes and at least 10 expected failures,

np^≥10andn(1−p^)≥10n\hat p \ge 10 \qquad \text{and} \qquad n(1-\hat p) \ge 10np^​≥10andn(1−p^​)≥10

(using p0p_0p0​ in place of p^\hat pp^​ for a hypothesis test — more on that below). You also need independence: the sample was drawn randomly, and if it was drawn without replacement from a finite population, the sample is no more than about 10% of that population. Skip the check and the whole Normal-curve shortcut — interval or test — is not trustworthy, no matter how clean the rest of the arithmetic looks.

2A confidence interval for one proportion¶

A confidence interval for ppp takes the same shape as every interval since Week 10: point estimate ± margin of error, where the margin of error is a critical value z∗z^*z∗ times the standard error:

p^  ±  z∗p^(1−p^)n\hat p \;\pm\; z^*\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n}}p^​±z∗np^​(1−p^​)​​

Note the standard error above uses p^\hat pp^​, not ppp — the whole point of the interval is that ppp is unknown, so p^\hat pp^​ is the best available stand-in. The familiar critical values return unchanged: z∗=1.645z^*=1.645z∗=1.645 for 90% confidence, z∗=1.96z^*=1.96z∗=1.96 for 95%, z∗=2.576z^*=2.576z∗=2.576 for 99%.

Worked Example 1 — are adopted pets coming back? A local animal shelter reviews its records for the past year: of n=250n=250n=250 pets adopted, x=34x=34x=34 were returned to the shelter within that first year.

p^=34250=0.136\hat p = \frac{34}{250} = 0.136p^​=25034​=0.136

Condition check. np^=250(0.136)=34≥10n\hat p = 250(0.136)=34\ge10np^​=250(0.136)=34≥10 and n(1−p^)=216≥10n(1-\hat p)=216\ge10n(1−p^​)=216≥10 — both comfortably clear the success–failure bar. The 250 adoption records were pulled at random from the shelter’s full adoption log and represent well under 10% of “every pet ever adopted,” so independence is reasonable too.

Building the 95% interval.

SE=0.136(0.864)250=0.0217ME=1.96(0.0217)=0.0425SE = \sqrt{\frac{0.136(0.864)}{250}} = 0.0217 \qquad ME = 1.96(0.0217) = 0.0425SE=2500.136(0.864)​​=0.0217ME=1.96(0.0217)=0.0425
0.136±0.0425  ⟶  (0.094, 0.178)0.136 \pm 0.0425 \;\longrightarrow\; (0.094,\ 0.178)0.136±0.0425⟶(0.094, 0.178)

Interpretation. We are 95% confident that the true proportion of all adopted pets at this shelter that get returned within a year is between 9.4% and 17.8%. That is a statement about the method, not this one interval: if the shelter repeated this sampling process many times, about 95% of the resulting intervals would capture the true return rate. It is not correct to say “there’s a 95% probability the true rate is in this interval” — the true rate is a fixed number; it’s the interval that varies from sample to sample.

Figure 1. A number line from 0 to 0.30 marking the point estimate p^=0.136\hat p=0.136p^​=0.136 with a dot and a horizontal bracket spanning from 0.094 to 0.178, representing the 95% confidence interval; the bracket sits entirely below 0.20, showing the true return rate is unlikely to reach 20%.

3Testing a claimed proportion: the one-proportion z-test¶

A hypothesis test starts from a specific claimed value, p0p_0p0​, and asks whether the sample data give good evidence against it. Following Week 11’s logic exactly:

H0:p=p0Ha:p≠p0 (or <p0 or >p0)H_0: p = p_0 \qquad\qquad H_a: p \ne p_0 \ \text{(or } < p_0 \text{ or } > p_0\text{)}H0​:p=p0​Ha​:p=p0​ (or <p0​ or >p0​)

Here is the one new wrinkle: the standard error in the test statistic uses p0p_0p0​, not p^\hat pp^​:

z=p^−p0SE0whereSE0=p0(1−p0)nz = \frac{\hat p - p_0}{SE_0} \qquad\text{where}\qquad SE_0 = \sqrt{\frac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}z=SE0​p^​−p0​​whereSE0​=np0​(1−p0​)​​

Why p0p_0p0​ and not p^\hat pp^​? A hypothesis test is a “what if” argument: if H0H_0H0​ were true and the population proportion really were p0p_0p0​, how surprising would a sample proportion this far from p0p_0p0​ be? Answering that question means describing the sampling distribution of p^\hat pp^​ under the assumption that p0p_0p0​ is correct — so every piece of that distribution, including its spread, is built from p0p_0p0​. Plugging in p^\hat pp^​ instead would quietly assume the very thing being tested. (This is also why the success–failure condition for a test checks np0≥10np_0\ge10np0​≥10 and n(1−p0)≥10n(1-p_0)\ge10n(1−p0​)≥10, not np^n\hat pnp^​.) The confidence interval has no such “what if” — there is no claimed value to test against — so it uses the only proportion it has, p^\hat pp^​.

Once you have zzz, get the p-value the same way as any z-test: the area in the tail(s) of the standard Normal curve beyond zzz (both tails for ≠\ne=, one tail for <<< or >>>). Compare the p-value to α\alphaα and decide.

Worked Example 2 — is the 90% commuter claim right? A campus flyer claims “90% of CSUB students commute rather than live on campus” (p0=0.90p_0=0.90p0​=0.90). Student government surveys a random sample of n=120n=120n=120 students; x=100x=100x=100 say they commute. Test the claim at α=0.05\alpha=0.05α=0.05.

H0:p=0.90Ha:p≠0.90H_0: p = 0.90 \qquad H_a: p \ne 0.90H0​:p=0.90Ha​:p=0.90

Condition check. np0=120(0.90)=108≥10np_0 = 120(0.90)=108\ge10np0​=120(0.90)=108≥10 and n(1−p0)=12≥10n(1-p_0)=12\ge10n(1−p0​)=12≥10 — condition holds. The sample was drawn at random, and 120 is far less than 10% of CSUB’s enrollment.

p^=100120=0.833SE0=0.90(0.10)120=0.0274\hat p = \frac{100}{120} = 0.833 \qquad SE_0 = \sqrt{\frac{0.90(0.10)}{120}} = 0.0274p^​=120100​=0.833SE0​=1200.90(0.10)​​=0.0274
z=0.833−0.900.0274=−2.43z = \frac{0.833 - 0.90}{0.0274} = -2.43z=0.02740.833−0.90​=−2.43

The two-sided p-value is the combined area beyond z=±2.43z=\pm2.43z=±2.43 under N(0,1)N(0,1)N(0,1): p-value ≈0.0149\approx 0.0149≈0.0149 (about 1.5%).

Decision. Since 0.0149<0.050.0149 < 0.050.0149<0.05, we reject H0H_0H0​. There is statistically significant evidence that the true proportion of CSUB students who commute is different from the claimed 90% — this sample’s 83.3% commuter rate is farther below 90% than we’d expect from sampling variability alone if 90% were really correct.

Running prop.test(34, 250, conf.level = 0.95) reports p^=0.136\hat p = 0.136p^​=0.136 and a 95% interval of (0.0973, 0.1863)(0.0973,\ 0.1863)(0.0973, 0.1863) — close to, but not identical to, the by-hand (0.094, 0.178)(0.094,\ 0.178)(0.094, 0.178) above. prop.test(100, 120, p = 0.90, alternative = "two.sided") reports z2z^2z2 (as a chi-squared statistic) and p-value =0.0225=0.0225=0.0225 — close to, but not identical to, the by-hand 0.0149.

Why the small gap? By default, R’s prop.test() applies a continuity correction. It also computes a slightly different interval formula, called a “Wilson score” interval, instead of the p^±z∗SE\hat p\pm z^*SEp^​±z∗SE formula this course teaches by hand and on the TI-84. Adding correct = FALSE moves R’s numbers closer to the hand calculation. Either way, R agrees with the hand calculation on what matters for a decision — reject or fail to reject, and roughly where the interval sits. That agreement is the whole point of using R as a confirmation tool: it is outstanding at matching careful hand work, and a poor substitute for understanding why the numbers came out that way. Two correct methods can report slightly different decimals — recognizing that, instead of assuming one of them must be wrong, is part of learning the limits of your tools.

MATH 952 skills refresher — count to proportion, then round sensibly

p^=x/n\hat p = x/np^​=x/n is one division: count the successes, count the sample, divide. For the shelter example, 34÷25034\div25034÷250 on a calculator gives 0.136 exactly — no rounding needed yet. Round only at the end, after computing SESESE and MEMEME from the full-precision p^\hat pp^​; rounding p^\hat pp^​ early (say, to 0.14) and carrying that rounded number through the rest of the formula quietly shifts the final interval. A quick sanity check: p^\hat pp^​ must always land between 0 and 1 (it’s a proportion) — if your division gives something outside that range, you divided the wrong way round.

5Key terms¶

  • Population proportion, ppp — the true share of an entire population that has some trait; a parameter, usually unknown.

  • Sample proportion, p^\hat pp^​ — x/nx/nx/n, the share of a sample with the trait; a statistic that estimates ppp.

  • Success–failure condition — the check np^≥10n\hat p \ge 10np^​≥10 and n(1−p^)≥10n(1-\hat p)\ge10n(1−p^​)≥10 (or np0≥10np_0\ge10np0​≥10, n(1−p0)≥10n(1-p_0)\ge10n(1−p0​)≥10 for a test) that must hold before treating the sampling distribution of p^\hat pp^​ as approximately Normal.

  • Null proportion, p0p_0p0​ — the specific value of ppp claimed under H0H_0H0​; used, not p^\hat pp^​, inside the test’s standard error.

  • One-proportion zzz-interval — p^±z∗p^(1−p^)/n\hat p \pm z^*\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}p^​±z∗p^​(1−p^​)/n​, a confidence interval for a single population proportion.

  • One-proportion zzz-test — a hypothesis test of H0:p=p0H_0: p=p_0H0​:p=p0​ using z=(p^−p0)/p0(1−p0)/nz=(\hat p-p_0)/\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}z=(p^​−p0​)/p0​(1−p0​)/n​.

Durable skill: quantitative reasoning & quantitative communication

Every “X% of customers/patients/voters” headline you’ll meet after graduation is exactly this week’s p^\hat pp^​, built from some sample you never see the details of. Knowing to ask “how big was the sample, and does it even clear the success–failure bar?” before trusting a percentage — and being able to report a claim as “we’re 95% confident the true rate is between 9% and 18%,” not just “about 14%” — is a portable skill for reading a market survey, a satisfaction report, or a clinical statistic with the same honest skepticism you’re practicing here.
