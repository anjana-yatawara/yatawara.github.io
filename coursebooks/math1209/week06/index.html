Week 6 — The Normal Model (setup)

This week in one line: you’ll meet the Normal curve, use the 68–95–99.7 rule to estimate proportions without calculus, and learn the z-score, a single number for “how far from typical.”

Look back at last week’s density curves — the smooth outline you sketched over a histogram of adult heights or test scores. Most of those curves shared one particular shape: a single hump, tallest in the middle, tapering off evenly on both sides like a bell. That shape is common enough, and useful enough, that it gets its own name and its own mathematical model — the Normal distribution.

This week you’ll learn to read that curve, use a simple rule to estimate proportions from it without any calculus, and start measuring “how far from typical” a single value really is.

1The Normal (bell) curve¶

A Normal distribution is a specific bell-shaped density curve: symmetric (the left half is a mirror image of the right half) and unimodal (one hump, no second peak). Unlike the shelter or coffee-shop tables from Week 3, a Normal curve isn’t built by counting categories — it’s a smooth model for a numerical variable, and it’s completely described by just two numbers:

  • μ\muμ (the Greek letter “mu”) — the mean, which sits exactly at the curve’s center and its peak.

  • σ\sigmaσ (the Greek letter “sigma”) — the standard deviation, which controls how wide or narrow the bell is. A small σ\sigmaσ makes a tall, narrow bell (values cluster tightly around μ\muμ); a large σ\sigmaσ makes a short, wide bell (values spread out).

Statisticians write X∼N(μ,σ)X \sim N(\mu, \sigma)X∼N(μ,σ) to mean “the variable XXX follows a Normal model with mean μ\muμ and standard deviation σ\sigmaσ.” Every Normal curve — tall and narrow or short and wide — has the same overall shape; only μ\muμ and σ\sigmaσ change where it’s centered and how spread out it is.

Why this particular shape keeps showing up. Heights, birth weights, measurement errors, and standardized test scores all tend to look approximately Normal. The reason is that each of these values is the net result of many small, independent factors adding together (genes, environment, timing, rounding error). It turns out that whenever you add up a lot of small independent effects, the result tends toward this exact bell shape — a fact called the Central Limit Theorem, which you’ll meet properly in Week 9. For now, just trust the pattern: it’s why the Normal model is worth learning well.

Figure 1. A single bell-shaped curve, tallest and centered at μ\muμ, falling away symmetrically on both sides and never quite touching the horizontal axis. Two reference curves are sketched faintly behind it: one taller and narrower (smaller σ\sigmaσ) and one shorter and wider (larger σ\sigmaσ), both centered at the same μ\muμ, showing that σ\sigmaσ controls spread while μ\muμ only shifts the center.

2The empirical rule: 68–95–99.7¶

Because every Normal curve has the same shape, the proportion of values within a given number of standard deviations of the mean is always the same, no matter what μ\muμ and σ\sigmaσ are. This fact is the empirical rule (also called the 68–95–99.7 rule):

  • About 68% of values fall within 1 SD of the mean: the interval [μ−σ, μ+σ][\mu-\sigma,\ \mu+\sigma][μ−σ, μ+σ].

  • About 95% of values fall within 2 SD of the mean: the interval [μ−2σ, μ+2σ][\mu-2\sigma,\ \mu+2\sigma][μ−2σ, μ+2σ].

  • About 99.7% of values fall within 3 SD of the mean: the interval [μ−3σ, μ+3σ][\mu-3\sigma,\ \mu+3\sigma][μ−3σ, μ+3σ].

Running the exact Normal model in R (see See it in R below) confirms these are rounded numbers: the precise areas are 68.27%68.27\%68.27%, 95.45%95.45\%95.45%, and 99.73%99.73\%99.73%. Because the curve is symmetric, whatever percentage is left over after each interval splits evenly between the two tails — e.g., 100−95=5%100-95=5\%100−95=5% left over at ±2\pm 2±2 SD means 2.5%2.5\%2.5% in each tail (exactly: 2.28%2.28\%2.28% each, from 95.45%95.45\%95.45%).

Worked Example 1 — Cat weights. Suppose, for this example, adult indoor-cat body weight is modeled as Normal with mean μ=10\mu = 10μ=10 lb and standard deviation σ=1.5\sigma = 1.5σ=1.5 lb (a stated model for practice, not a fitted real dataset — the exact Normal fit for a real shelter population is a job for later coursework). Using the empirical rule:

SDs from meanIntervalApprox. % of cats
1[8.5, 11.5][8.5,\ 11.5][8.5, 11.5] lb68%
2[7, 13][7,\ 13][7, 13] lb95%
3[5.5, 14.5][5.5,\ 14.5][5.5, 14.5] lb99.7%

So about 68% of cats weigh between 8.5 and 11.5 lb, and only about 2.5%2.5\%2.5% weigh more than 13 lb (2 SD above the mean) — a genuinely heavy cat by this model. A cat weighing exactly 12 lb is (12−10)/1.5≈1.33(12-10)/1.5 \approx 1.33(12−10)/1.5≈1.33 SDs above the mean — not a round grid line, so the empirical rule alone can’t give an exact percentage. That’s exactly the gap Week 8’s tools close: R’s xpnorm reports the precise answer, P(X>12)≈9.12%P(X>12) \approx 9.12\%P(X>12)≈9.12%, meaning about 9 in 100 cats in this model weigh more than 12 lb.

3Standardizing: the z-score¶

A z-score answers one question in one number: how many standard deviations is a value from the mean, and in which direction?

z=x−μσz = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}z=σx−μ​

Subtract the mean, then divide by the standard deviation — one subtraction, one division. A positive zzz means xxx is above the mean; negative means below; z=0z=0z=0 means xxx is the mean exactly. A value with z=±1,±2,±3z=\pm1,\pm2,\pm3z=±1,±2,±3 sits right on one of the empirical-rule grid lines above; any other zzz falls between grid lines, which is where a tool like xpnorm is most useful. Standardizing a whole distribution means converting every value to its z-score, which always produces the standard Normal distribution, N(0,1)N(0,1)N(0,1) — mean 0, SD 1 — the same reference curve no matter what the original μ\muμ and σ\sigmaσ were. That shared reference curve is exactly what lets you compare values from two different Normal models on equal footing.

Worked Example 2 — Commute times. Suppose commute times to campus are modeled as Normal with μ=32\mu=32μ=32 minutes and σ=8\sigma=8σ=8 minutes. A commute of 40 minutes is exactly 1 SD above the mean (z=1z=1z=1), so the empirical rule says about 15.87%15.87\%15.87% of commutes are longer than 40 minutes. A commute of 45 minutes gives z=(45−32)/8=1.625z=(45-32)/8=1.625z=(45−32)/8=1.625 — between the 1-SD and 2-SD grid lines. The empirical rule can only say “somewhere between 15.87% and 2.28%”; xpnorm gives the exact figure, P(X>45)≈5.21%P(X>45)\approx5.21\%P(X>45)≈5.21%.

Worked Example 3 — Comparing across two different Normal models. Suppose (again, a stated model for practice) ACT composite scores follow N(21,5)N(21,5)N(21,5) and SAT total scores follow N(1050,200)N(1050,200)N(1050,200) — the ACT and SAT are two US college-entrance exams, each scored on its own scale. Student A scores a 27 on the ACT; Student B scores a 1250 on the SAT. Raw scores aren’t comparable — a 27 and a 1250 live on completely different scales — but their z-scores are:

zA=27−215=1.2zB=1250−1050200=1.0z_A = \frac{27-21}{5} = 1.2 \qquad\qquad z_B = \frac{1250-1050}{200} = 1.0zA​=527−21​=1.2zB​=2001250−1050​=1.0

Student A’s score sits 1.2 SDs above the ACT mean; Student B’s sits 1.0 SD above the SAT mean. Because 1.2>1.01.2 > 1.01.2>1.0, Student A’s score is relatively higher — farther above typical within its own distribution — even though 1250 is a much bigger raw number than 27. (A related question you’ll answer precisely starting in Week 8: what raw score marks the cutoff for, say, the top 10% of test-takers? That needs invNorm/xqnorm, run backwards from a percentage to a value — OpenIntro Chapter 4.)

MATH 952 skills refresher — subtract, then divide, one step at a time

A z-score is two arithmetic steps done in order: first subtract (x−μx-\mux−μ), then divide by σ\sigmaσ. Skipping the parentheses and dividing first is the most common error. For the 12-lb cat: 12−10=212-10=212−10=2, then 2÷1.5=1.333‾2 \div 1.5 = 1.33\overline{3}2÷1.5=1.333. On a number line marked at μ=10\mu=10μ=10 with tick marks every σ=1.5\sigma=1.5σ=1.5 (so ticks fall at 8.5,10,11.5,13,…8.5,10,11.5,13,\ldots8.5,10,11.5,13,…), 12 lands between the 11.5 and 13 ticks — visually confirming a z-score a little above 1 but below 2, before you even compute it exactly.

5Key terms¶

  • Normal distribution — a symmetric, unimodal, bell-shaped density curve, fully described by its mean μ\muμ and standard deviation σ\sigmaσ; written N(μ,σ)N(\mu,\sigma)N(μ,σ).

  • Symmetric — the left half of a distribution mirrors the right half.

  • Unimodal — having exactly one peak (hump).

  • Empirical rule (68–95–99.7 rule) — for any Normal model, about 68% of values fall within 1 SD of the mean, 95% within 2 SD, and 99.7% within 3 SD.

  • z-score — z=(x−μ)/σz=(x-\mu)/\sigmaz=(x−μ)/σ; how many standard deviations xxx is from the mean, and in which direction (positive = above, negative = below).

  • Standardizing — converting a value (or a whole distribution) to z-scores so it can be compared on the same scale as any other Normal distribution.

  • Standard Normal distribution, N(0,1)N(0,1)N(0,1) — the Normal model with mean 0 and SD 1; what any Normal distribution becomes once standardized.

Durable skill: quantitative reasoning & quantitative communication

“How unusual is this?” is a question that comes up everywhere outside the classroom — a lab value flagged on a medical chart, a factory part outside spec, a sales month that beats the average. The z-score is a small, portable tool for turning that question into one honest number, and being able to say “this is about 1.3 SDs above typical” instead of just “this seems high” is exactly the kind of precise, defensible communication employers and clinicians rely on.
