:::{admonition} The Opening Problem
:class: important

**The Prosecutor's Fallacy.** In 1999, British solicitor Sally Clark was convicted of murdering her two infant sons. The prosecution's key evidence? Pediatrician Sir Roy Meadow testified that the probability of two children in the same family dying of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) was 1 in 73 million — so it *must* have been murder.

The jury convicted. Sally Clark went to prison.

But the statistic was catastrophically wrong. Meadow had confused two very different questions: *"What is the probability that two children die of SIDS?"* versus *"What is the probability that a mother is innocent, given that two children died?"* The first is $P(\text{evidence} | \text{innocent})$. The second is $P(\text{innocent} | \text{evidence})$. These are **not the same number** — and the difference between them is not a technicality. It is the difference between freedom and prison.

Meadow arrived at 1 in 73 million by squaring the single-SIDS rate of 1 in 8,543 — implicitly assuming the two deaths were *independent*. But SIDS deaths within a family share genetic and environmental risk factors. The actual conditional probability of a second SIDS death given the first was closer to 1 in 60–130, not 1 in 8,543. The Royal Statistical Society issued a formal public rebuke of the misuse of statistics in Clark's trial.

After a lengthy appeal, Clark was exonerated in January 2003 and released after more than three years in prison. She never recovered and died in 2007.

Understanding conditional probability is not just mathematics. It is a matter of justice, of life and death, and of getting the right answer when the stakes are highest. By the end of this chapter, you will understand exactly *why* the prosecution's argument was wrong — and you will have the mathematical tools to never make the same mistake.

## Conditional Probability

:::{admonition} What Are We Going to Learn?
:class: note

**The big idea:** Most probabilities in real life come with strings attached. When a doctor says *"the probability that this treatment works,"* they mean for patients *like you* — your age, your condition, your medical history. That's a **conditional probability**: the probability of one event, *given that we already know* another event has occurred.

**Why this matters right now:** A basketball player shoots free throws at 80% overall. But what's her percentage when the game is tied in the fourth quarter? That's $P(\text{make} \mid \text{tie game in Q4})$ — a conditional probability that might be very different from her overall 80%. Coaches, analysts, and sports bettors all need conditional probabilities, not unconditional ones, to make good decisions under pressure.

In this section, you will learn:

- How to formalize "probability given that something else happened"
- The definition of $P(A|B)$ and what every piece of it means
- How to compute conditional probabilities from tables, formulas, and Venn diagrams
- Why $P(A|B)$ and $P(B|A)$ are almost always different numbers

:::

### The Intuition: Shrinking the Sample Space

Let's start with a concrete situation before we write any formulas.

A local coffee shop tracked 500 customers over a week and recorded two things: whether the customer ordered a pastry, and whether the customer visited in the morning (before noon) or afternoon.

|  | Morning | Afternoon | **Total** |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Ordered a pastry | 140 | 60 | **200** |
| No pastry | 160 | 140 | **300** |
| **Total** | **300** | **200** | **500** |

Suppose we pick a customer at random. What is the probability they ordered a pastry?

$$P(\text{pastry}) = \frac{200}{500} = 0.40.$$

Now here's the twist. Someone tells you: *"The customer visited in the morning."* Does this change the probability they ordered a pastry?

**Yes, it does.** If we *know* the customer visited in the morning, we're no longer looking at all 500 customers. We've **shrunk the sample space** to the 300 morning customers. Among those 300, how many ordered a pastry? It's 140. So:

$$P(\text{pastry} \mid \text{morning}) = \frac{140}{300} = \frac{7}{15} \approx 0.467.$$

That vertical bar "$\mid$" is read *"given."* The expression $P(\text{pastry} \mid \text{morning})$ means: "the probability of a pastry order, *given that* we know the customer came in the morning."

Notice what happened. The **overall** probability of ordering a pastry is 0.40. But among **morning** customers, it jumps to about 0.467. Knowing the time of visit changed our assessment. That's what conditioning does — it updates your probability by restricting your attention to a subset of outcomes.

### The Formal Definition

Let's translate the intuition into mathematics. Look again at what we computed:

$$P(\text{pastry} \mid \text{morning}) = \frac{140}{300}.$$

Divide both the numerator and denominator by 500 (the total number of customers):

$$P(\text{pastry} \mid \text{morning}) = \frac{140/500}{300/500} = \frac{P(\text{pastry} \cap \text{morning})}{P(\text{morning})}.$$

This is the general pattern.

:::{admonition} Definition 3.1: Conditional Probability
:class: important

If $A$ and $B$ are events with $P(B) > 0$, the **conditional probability of $A$ given $B$** is

$$P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}.$$

:::

Three things to notice immediately:

1. **The denominator is $P(B)$, not $P(S)$.** Conditioning on $B$ means $B$ becomes our new universe. Everything gets rescaled so that $P(B|B) = 1$.

2. **$P(B)$ must be positive.** If $P(B) = 0$, the definition doesn't apply — you can't condition on an event that never happens. (Division by zero is undefined.)

3. **$P(A|B)$ is itself a legitimate probability.** It satisfies all three axioms of probability. This means every rule you learned in Chapter 2 — complements, unions, inclusion-exclusion — works inside a conditional universe too. We'll prove this next.

### Conditional Probability Satisfies the Axioms

:::{admonition} Theorem 3.1: Conditional Probability Is a Valid Probability
:class: note

If $P(B) > 0$, then the function $P(\cdot | B)$ satisfies the axioms of probability:

1. $P(A|B) \geq 0$ for any event $A$.
2. $P(B|B) = 1$.
3. If $A_1, A_2, \ldots$ are mutually exclusive events, then $P\!\left(\bigcup_{i} A_i \,\middle|\, B\right) = \sum_{i} P(A_i | B)$.

**Proof.**

**Strategy:** We verify each axiom directly from Definition 3.1. The key idea is that ratios of non-negative numbers are non-negative, and the mutual exclusivity of the $A_i$ transfers through intersection with $B$.

**Axiom 1:** We need $P(A|B) \geq 0$. Since $P(A \cap B) \geq 0$ (by Axiom 1 for regular probability) and $P(B) > 0$ (given), their ratio is non-negative:

$$P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} \geq 0. \quad \checkmark$$

**Axiom 2:** We need $P(B|B) = 1$. Since $B \cap B = B$:

$$P(B|B) = \frac{P(B \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{P(B)}{P(B)} = 1. \quad \checkmark$$

**Axiom 3:** We need the countable additivity property. Suppose $A_1, A_2, \ldots$ are mutually exclusive (i.e., $A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset$ for $i \neq j$).

*First, we show that the sets $(A_1 \cap B), (A_2 \cap B), \ldots$ are also mutually exclusive:* If $A_i \cap A_j = \emptyset$, then

$$(A_i \cap B) \cap (A_j \cap B) = (A_i \cap A_j) \cap B = \emptyset \cap B = \emptyset.$$

*Next, we use the distributive law* $(\bigcup_i A_i) \cap B = \bigcup_i (A_i \cap B)$ to write:

$$P\!\left(\bigcup_{i} A_i \,\middle|\, B\right) = \frac{P\!\left(\left(\bigcup_{i} A_i\right) \cap B\right)}{P(B)} = \frac{P\!\left(\bigcup_{i} (A_i \cap B)\right)}{P(B)}.$$

*Now, since the $(A_i \cap B)$ are mutually exclusive*, Axiom 3 for regular probability gives us:

$$= \frac{\sum_{i} P(A_i \cap B)}{P(B)} = \sum_{i} \frac{P(A_i \cap B)}{P(B)} = \sum_{i} P(A_i | B). \quad \blacksquare$$

**Why this matters:** This theorem is your license to use everything from Chapter 2 inside a conditional world. For instance:

- $P(A^c | B) = 1 - P(A|B)$ (complement rule)
- $P(A_1 \cup A_2 | B) = P(A_1|B) + P(A_2|B) - P(A_1 \cap A_2 | B)$ (inclusion-exclusion)

These aren't new formulas. They're the old formulas applied in the $B$-universe.

:::

### Worked Examples

**EXAMPLE 3.1: Quality Control at a Bottling Plant**

A bottling plant inspects bottles for two types of defects: *underfilling* (less than the labeled volume) and *mislabeling* (wrong label applied). Records show:

- $P(\text{underfill}) = 0.08$
- $P(\text{mislabel}) = 0.05$
- $P(\text{underfill} \cap \text{mislabel}) = 0.02$

A bottle is pulled off the line and found to be mislabeled. What is the probability that it is also underfilled?

**Solution.**

Step 1: Identify what we need. We want $P(\text{underfill} \mid \text{mislabel})$.

Step 2: Apply the definition.

$$P(\text{underfill} \mid \text{mislabel}) = \frac{P(\text{underfill} \cap \text{mislabel})}{P(\text{mislabel})} = \frac{0.02}{0.05} = 0.40.$$

Step 3: Interpret. Among mislabeled bottles, **40%** are also underfilled. Compare this to the overall underfill rate of 8%. Knowing a bottle is mislabeled dramatically increases the chance it's underfilled — perhaps both defects share a common cause (a malfunctioning machine).

:::{admonition} Common Mistake: Intersection vs. Conditional
:class: warning

Students often compute $P(A \cap B)$ when asked for $P(A | B)$. These are different questions:

- $P(\text{underfill} \cap \text{mislabel}) = 0.02$ → "What fraction of ALL bottles have BOTH defects?"
- $P(\text{underfill} | \text{mislabel}) = 0.40$ → "Among MISLABELED bottles, what fraction are also underfilled?"

The denominator changes the universe you're counting in.

:::

**EXAMPLE 3.2: The Asymmetry That Destroys Courtroom Arguments**

An auto insurance company classifies 1,000 policyholders:

|  | Filed a Claim | No Claim | **Total** |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Under 25 | 75 | 125 | **200** |
| 25 and over | 150 | 650 | **800** |
| **Total** | **225** | **775** | **1000** |

Compute both $P(\text{claim} | \text{under 25})$ and $P(\text{under 25} | \text{claim})$.

**Solution.**

$$P(\text{claim} \mid \text{under 25}) = \frac{75/1000}{200/1000} = \frac{75}{200} = 0.375.$$

$$P(\text{under 25} \mid \text{claim}) = \frac{75/1000}{225/1000} = \frac{75}{225} = 0.333.$$

**These are not the same.** The first says: "Among drivers under 25, 37.5% file a claim." The second says: "Among all claimants, 33.3% are under 25." Different questions, different denominators, different answers.

This is the exact confusion that destroyed Sally Clark's life. $P(\text{evidence} \mid \text{innocent})$ is not the same as $P(\text{innocent} \mid \text{evidence})$. Getting these backwards has a name: the **Prosecutor's Fallacy** (also called the **fallacy of the transposed conditional** or **confusion of the inverse**). We'll resolve it formally in Section 3.5 with Bayes' Rule.

**EXAMPLE 3.3: Drawing Without Replacement**

A box contains 8 red balls and 4 blue balls. Two balls are drawn one at a time *without replacement*. What is the probability that the second ball is red, given that the first ball was blue?

**Solution.**

Let $R_2$ = "second ball is red" and $B_1$ = "first ball is blue."

After drawing a blue ball first, the box contains 8 red and 3 blue balls — a total of 11 balls. So:

$$P(R_2 | B_1) = \frac{8}{11} \approx 0.727.$$

This is *higher* than the unconditional probability $P(R_2) = 8/12 = 2/3 \approx 0.667$. Why? Because removing a blue ball on the first draw left a higher proportion of red balls remaining.

We can verify this with the formal definition. With 12 balls total and two draws without replacement, there are $12 \times 11 = 132$ ordered outcomes.

$$P(B_1 \cap R_2) = \frac{4 \times 8}{12 \times 11} = \frac{32}{132}, \qquad P(B_1) = \frac{4}{12} = \frac{44}{132}.$$

$$P(R_2 | B_1) = \frac{32/132}{44/132} = \frac{32}{44} = \frac{8}{11}. \quad \checkmark$$

**IN R: Simulation**

```r
# Simulate drawing without replacement
set.seed(42)
n_sim <- 100000
box <- c(rep("red", 8), rep("blue", 4))

first_blue <- 0; second_red_given_first_blue <- 0
for (i in 1:n_sim) {
  draw <- sample(box, 2, replace = FALSE)
  if (draw[1] == "blue") {
    first_blue <- first_blue + 1
    if (draw[2] == "red") second_red_given_first_blue <- second_red_given_first_blue + 1
  }
}
cat("Simulated P(R2 | B1) =", round(second_red_given_first_blue / first_blue, 4), "\n")
cat("Theoretical P(R2 | B1) = 8/11 =", round(8/11, 4), "\n")
```

**EXAMPLE 3.4: The Monty Hall Problem — Solved Formally**

In Chapter 1, we introduced the Monty Hall Problem and claimed that switching wins 2/3 of the time. Now we have the tools to prove it.

**Setup.** Three doors. A car is behind one (chosen uniformly at random); goats behind the other two. You pick Door 1. The host (Monty), who knows where the car is, opens a door revealing a goat — say he opens Door 3. Should you switch to Door 2?

Let $C_i$ = "car is behind door $i$" and $D_3$ = "Monty opens door 3." We want $P(C_2 | D_3)$ — the probability that the car is behind Door 2, given that Monty opened Door 3.

**The key probabilities we need:**

- $P(C_1) = P(C_2) = P(C_3) = 1/3$ (car is equally likely behind any door).
- $P(D_3 | C_1) = 1/2$ (if the car is behind Door 1, Monty can open either Door 2 or Door 3; assume he picks uniformly).
- $P(D_3 | C_2) = 1$ (if the car is behind Door 2, Monty *must* open Door 3 — it's the only goat door he can open, since you picked Door 1).
- $P(D_3 | C_3) = 0$ (if the car is behind Door 3, Monty can't open Door 3 — he'd reveal the car).

**Apply the definition of conditional probability:**

$$P(C_2 | D_3) = \frac{P(D_3 | C_2) \cdot P(C_2)}{P(D_3)}.$$

We need $P(D_3)$ by the Law of Total Probability (Section 3.4 — we're previewing it here):

$$P(D_3) = P(D_3|C_1)P(C_1) + P(D_3|C_2)P(C_2) + P(D_3|C_3)P(C_3) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{3} + 1 \cdot \frac{1}{3} + 0 \cdot \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{2}.$$

Therefore:

$$P(C_2 | D_3) = \frac{1 \cdot \frac{1}{3}}{\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{2}{3}.$$

**Switching wins with probability $\boldsymbol{2/3}$.** Staying wins with probability $P(C_1 | D_3) = 1/3$.

Why does switching work? Because Monty's action is *informative*. When the car is behind Door 2, Monty is *forced* to open Door 3 (probability 1). When the car is behind Door 1, Monty opens Door 3 only half the time. So observing "Monty opened Door 3" is *twice as likely* under $C_2$ as under $C_1$. Conditioning on Monty's choice shifts the probability.

:::{admonition} What Did We Learn?
:class: note

**Conditional probability is regular probability with a smaller universe.** The formula $P(A|B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)$ says: restrict your attention to outcomes where $B$ happened, and measure how much of that restricted world also has $A$.

**The real-life takeaway:** Almost every probability you encounter in practice is conditional on *something*. The unconditional probability is often the least useful number. The skill is recognizing *what you're conditioning on*.

**The critical warning:** $P(A|B) \neq P(B|A)$ in general. The Prosecutor's Fallacy is the error of treating these as interchangeable. Bayes' Rule (Section 3.5) is the antidote.

## Independence of Events

:::{admonition} What Are We Going to Learn?
:class: note

**The big idea:** Sometimes, knowing that one event occurred tells you *nothing* about whether another event occurred. The weather in Tokyo today has no effect on whether your coin lands heads. When knowing $B$ doesn't change the probability of $A$, we say $A$ and $B$ are **independent**.

**Why this matters right now:** In 2008, the global financial system nearly collapsed. One major cause: banks priced mortgage-backed securities by assuming individual mortgage defaults were *nearly independent*. They weren't — when the housing market turned, defaults happened together across entire regions. The mathematical model (David X. Li's Gaussian copula) used a single correlation parameter calibrated on benign historical data. When defaults stopped being independent, trillions of dollars in "safe" assets became worthless overnight. Independence is an assumption — and getting it wrong can be catastrophic.

In this section, you will learn:

- The formal definition of independence and how to verify it
- Why independence is *not* the same as "mutually exclusive"
- How to extend independence to three or more events
- When independence is a reasonable assumption and when it isn't

:::

### When Conditioning Changes Nothing

Look back at Definition 3.1. If $P(A|B) = P(A)$, then learning $B$ occurred provides *zero information* about $A$. The probability of $A$ is the same whether $B$ happened or not.

What does this imply? If $P(A|B) = P(A)$, then:

$$\frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = P(A) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B).$$

This is symmetric — if $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$ and $P(A) > 0$, then:

$$P(B|A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)} = \frac{P(A) \cdot P(B)}{P(A)} = P(B).$$

So if $A$ doesn't affect $B$, then $B$ doesn't affect $A$ either. Independence is a two-way street.

:::{admonition} Definition 3.2: Independence of Two Events
:class: important

Two events $A$ and $B$ are **independent** if

$$P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B).$$

Equivalently (when the conditioning is well-defined):

- $P(A|B) = P(A)$, or
- $P(B|A) = P(B)$.

Any one of these three conditions implies the other two.

:::

### Checking Independence: Verify, Don't Assume

**EXAMPLE 3.5: Weather and Transit — Not Independent**

A transportation survey of 1,000 commuters:

|  | Drove | Public Transit | **Total** |
|:---|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Rainy | 120 | 180 | **300** |
| Not rainy | 380 | 320 | **700** |
| **Total** | **500** | **500** | **1000** |

Let $R$ = "rainy day" and $T$ = "took public transit." Are $R$ and $T$ independent?

**Check:** $P(R) \cdot P(T) = 0.30 \times 0.50 = 0.15.$ But $P(R \cap T) = 180/1000 = 0.18.$

Since $0.18 \neq 0.15$, **not independent.** People are more likely to take transit when it rains.

**EXAMPLE 3.6: Two Dice — Surprisingly Independent**

Roll a fair die twice. Let $A$ = "first roll is 4" and $B$ = "sum of both rolls is 7."

$$P(A) = \frac{1}{6}, \quad P(B) = \frac{6}{36} = \frac{1}{6}, \quad P(A \cap B) = \frac{1}{36}.$$

(Only (4,3) has first die = 4 AND sum = 7.)

Check: $P(A) \cdot P(B) = \frac{1}{6} \cdot \frac{1}{6} = \frac{1}{36} = P(A \cap B).$ $\checkmark$

**Independent!** This might be surprising — the sum involves the first roll. But no matter what the first die shows, exactly one value of the second die makes the sum 7.

### Independence vs. Mutually Exclusive: The Great Confusion

:::{admonition} Common Mistake: "Independent" ≠ "Mutually Exclusive"
:class: warning

**Mutually exclusive** means $A \cap B = \emptyset$, so $P(A \cap B) = 0$. The events *cannot happen together.*

**Independent** means $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$. The events *don't influence each other.*

**These are nearly opposite.** If $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive and both have positive probability, then $P(A \cap B) = 0$ but $P(A) \cdot P(B) > 0$. So they are **not independent** — in fact, they are *maximally dependent.* If you know $A$ happened, you know *for certain* that $B$ did not happen: $P(B|A) = 0 \neq P(B)$.

**The slogan:** Mutually exclusive events are maximally dependent, not independent.

:::

### Independence of Three or More Events

For two events, one equation ($P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$) suffices. For three events, things get more demanding.

:::{admonition} Definition 3.3: Mutual Independence
:class: important

Events $A$, $B$, and $C$ are **mutually independent** if ALL FOUR of the following hold:

1. $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$
2. $P(A \cap C) = P(A) \cdot P(C)$
3. $P(B \cap C) = P(B) \cdot P(C)$
4. $P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A) \cdot P(B) \cdot P(C)$

Conditions 1–3 alone are called **pairwise independence** and are NOT sufficient for mutual independence. You need condition 4 as well.

:::

**EXAMPLE 3.7: Pairwise but Not Mutually Independent**

Toss a fair coin twice. Define:

- $A$ = "first toss is heads"
- $B$ = "second toss is heads"
- $C$ = "both tosses give the same result" (both H or both T)

Then $P(A) = P(B) = P(C) = 1/2$.

Check pairwise: $A \cap B$ = {HH}, so $P(A \cap B) = 1/4 = (1/2)(1/2)$. $\checkmark$ Similarly for $A \cap C$ and $B \cap C$.

But: $A \cap B \cap C$ = {HH}, so $P(A \cap B \cap C) = 1/4 \neq 1/8 = (1/2)^3$. $\boldsymbol{\times}$

**Pairwise independent but NOT mutually independent.** Knowing any *one* event tells you nothing about any other single event. But knowing *two* of them determines the third with certainty.

### When Independence Fails: Real-World Disasters

The most devastating failures of the independence assumption come from **common-cause failures** — when events that *look* independent share a hidden trigger.

**The Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Disaster (2011).** The plant had 13 emergency diesel generators, designed as a redundant backup system. Under an independence assumption, $P(\text{all 13 fail}) = (0.001)^{13}$ — effectively impossible. But the 9.0-magnitude Tōhoku earthquake triggered a 13–15 meter tsunami (the design basis was only 5.7 meters), which flooded the generator rooms and destroyed **12 of 13 generators simultaneously.** The single shared cause — the tsunami — made the "independent" backup systems fail together. This is called **common-cause failure**, and it's why engineers must always ask: "What shared event could knock out all of my redundancy at once?"

**The lesson for probability students:** The multiplication rule $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$ is only valid when $A$ and $B$ are truly independent. When events share a common cause, the joint probability can be *orders of magnitude* larger than the product suggests. Always justify the independence assumption — never just assume it.

:::{admonition} Theorem 3.2: Independence and Complements
:class: note

If $A$ and $B$ are independent, then so are $A$ and $B^c$, $A^c$ and $B$, and $A^c$ and $B^c$.

**Proof (for $A$ and $B^c$).**

**Strategy:** Express $P(A \cap B^c)$ using the fact that $A = (A \cap B) \cup (A \cap B^c)$ and apply the independence of $A$ and $B$.

*Step 1.* Since $B$ and $B^c$ partition $S$, and $(A \cap B)$ and $(A \cap B^c)$ are mutually exclusive:

$$P(A) = P(A \cap B) + P(A \cap B^c). \quad \text{(This is just Axiom 3.)}$$

*Step 2.* Solve for $P(A \cap B^c)$:

$$P(A \cap B^c) = P(A) - P(A \cap B).$$

*Step 3.* Now substitute $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$ (because $A$ and $B$ are independent):

$$P(A \cap B^c) = P(A) - P(A) \cdot P(B) = P(A)\big[1 - P(B)\big] = P(A) \cdot P(B^c).$$

*Step 4.* This is exactly the definition of independence for $A$ and $B^c$. $\blacksquare$

**Why this matters:** If "rain" and "traffic jam" are independent, then "no rain" and "traffic jam" are also independent. Independence is preserved under complementation — a useful shortcut.

:::

::::{admonition} Guided Practice 3.2
:class: tip

Two machines, $M_1$ and $M_2$, operate independently. $M_1$ breaks down with probability 0.04; $M_2$ with probability 0.06.

(b) $P(\text{at least one breaks down})$?
(c) $P(\text{exactly one breaks down})$?
(d) The factory operates if at most one machine is down. $P(\text{factory operates})$?

:::{admonition} Solution
:class: tip

(a) $P(A \cap B) = 0.04 \times 0.06 = 0.0024.$

(b) $P(A \cup B) = 1 - P(A^c \cap B^c) = 1 - (0.96)(0.94) = 1 - 0.9024 = 0.0976.$

(c) $P(\text{exactly one}) = P(A \cap B^c) + P(A^c \cap B) = (0.04)(0.94) + (0.96)(0.06) = 0.0376 + 0.0576 = 0.0952.$

(d) $P(\text{operates}) = 1 - P(\text{both fail}) = 1 - 0.0024 = 0.9976.$

:::{admonition} What Did We Learn?
:class: note

**Independence means "no information transfer."** If $A$ and $B$ are independent, observing $B$ doesn't change your probability for $A$. The mathematical signature is $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$.

**Independence ≠ mutually exclusive.** These are nearly opposite concepts.

**Independence is an assumption, not a fact.** The 2008 financial crisis and the Fukushima disaster both resulted from treating correlated events as independent. Always ask: *"What shared cause could make these events fail together?"*

## The Multiplication Rule

:::{admonition} What Are We Going to Learn?
:class: note

**The big idea:** Rearranging the conditional probability definition gives $P(A \cap B) = P(B) \cdot P(A|B)$ — a formula for "both $A$ and $B$" when you know one probability and the conditional probability of the other. This is the **multiplication rule**, and it handles any sequential process where each stage depends on the previous one.

**Why this matters right now:** Genetic counseling relies on this. If both parents carry the cystic fibrosis gene (each with probability 1), the multiplication rule on the Punnett square gives a 1/4 chance their child is affected. But the real question is: what's the probability a random couple *both* carry the gene *and* have an affected child? That chains three multiplication steps together — and the answer drives screening policy for millions of families.

:::

### The Rule

:::{admonition} Theorem 3.3: The Multiplication Rule
:class: important

For any two events $A$ and $B$:

$$P(A \cap B) = P(B) \cdot P(A|B) = P(A) \cdot P(B|A).$$

If $A$ and $B$ are independent, this simplifies to $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$.

**Proof.** This is simply Definition 3.1 rearranged. Start with $P(A|B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)$ and multiply both sides by $P(B)$. $\blacksquare$

:::

**Extension to three events:**

$$P(A \cap B \cap C) = P(A) \cdot P(B|A) \cdot P(C|A \cap B).$$

Read this as a chain: start with $P(A)$, then multiply by the probability of $B$ *given $A$ happened*, then multiply by the probability of $C$ *given both $A$ and $B$ happened*.

**General form for $k$ events:**

$$P(A_1 \cap A_2 \cap \cdots \cap A_k) = P(A_1) \cdot P(A_2|A_1) \cdot P(A_3|A_1 \cap A_2) \cdots P(A_k|A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_{k-1}).$$

:::{note}

**Proof of the three-event case.**

**Strategy:** Apply Theorem 3.3 twice — first to the pair $(A \cap B)$ and $C$, then to $A$ and $B$.

$$P(A \cap B \cap C) = P\big[(A \cap B) \cap C\big] = P(A \cap B) \cdot P(C | A \cap B).$$

*Now expand $P(A \cap B)$ using Theorem 3.3 again:*

$$= \big[P(A) \cdot P(B|A)\big] \cdot P(C|A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B|A) \cdot P(C|A \cap B). \quad \blacksquare$$

*Each factor in the chain "inherits" all the conditions from the previous steps. This is why tree diagrams work — each branch carries the accumulated conditions.*

:::

### Tree Diagrams: The Multiplication Rule Made Visual

**EXAMPLE 3.8: Airport Security Screening**

An airport checkpoint uses two phases. Phase 1: 85% pass, 15% flagged. Phase 2 (for flagged passengers only): 70% cleared, 30% detained.

$$P(\text{detained}) = P(\text{flagged}) \cdot P(\text{detained} | \text{flagged}) = 0.15 \times 0.30 = 0.045.$$

About 4.5% of passengers are detained.

**EXAMPLE 3.9: Genetics — Cystic Fibrosis Carrier Probability**

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is caused by a recessive allele. A carrier has genotype $Ff$ (one normal allele $F$, one CF allele $f$). If both parents are carriers ($Ff \times Ff$), the Punnett square gives:

| | $F$ (from dad) | $f$ (from dad) |
|:---|:---:|:---:|
| $F$ (from mom) | $FF$ (unaffected) | $Ff$ (carrier) |
| $f$ (from mom) | $Ff$ (carrier) | $ff$ (affected) |

Each cell has probability 1/4. So $P(\text{affected}) = 1/4$, $P(\text{carrier}) = 2/4 = 1/2$, $P(\text{unaffected, not carrier}) = 1/4$.

**Now the real-world question:** In the U.S. population of European descent, approximately 1 in 25 people are CF carriers. If two people from this population have a child, what is the probability the child is affected?

Using the multiplication rule with independent carrier status:

$$P(\text{child affected}) = P(\text{dad carrier}) \times P(\text{mom carrier}) \times P(\text{affected} | \text{both carriers}) = \frac{1}{25} \times \frac{1}{25} \times \frac{1}{4} = \frac{1}{2500}.$$

This matches the observed prevalence: approximately 1 in 2,500 live births.

**EXAMPLE 3.10: Three Cards Without Replacement**

Three cards are drawn from a standard 52-card deck, one at a time, without replacement. $P(\text{all three hearts})$?

$$P(H_1 \cap H_2 \cap H_3) = P(H_1) \cdot P(H_2|H_1) \cdot P(H_3|H_1 \cap H_2) = \frac{13}{52} \cdot \frac{12}{51} \cdot \frac{11}{50} = \frac{1716}{132600} = \frac{11}{850} \approx 0.0129.$$

About 1 in 77. Notice how each conditional probability adjusts for cards already drawn — this is *why* the multiplication rule uses conditional probabilities, not unconditional ones.

:class: note

The **multiplication rule** converts conditional probabilities into joint probabilities. It answers: "what is the probability that ALL of these things happen?" by chaining conditional probabilities along a sequence.

**Key insight:** Each multiplication by a number less than 1 makes the result smaller. Long chains of individually small failure rates can produce surprisingly high overall failure rates.

## The Law of Total Probability

:::{admonition} What Are We Going to Learn?
:class: note

**The big idea:** Sometimes $P(A)$ is hard to compute directly, but becomes easy if you first split the sample space into pieces and compute $P(A)$ within each piece. The **Law of Total Probability** tells you how to combine the pieces: it's a *weighted average* of conditional probabilities, not a simple average.

**Why this matters right now:** During the COVID-19 pandemic, data from Israel showed that **60% of severely hospitalized patients were fully vaccinated**, leading some to claim the vaccine didn't work. But this ignored the composition of the population: over 90% of older Israelis (who are most vulnerable) were vaccinated. Once you condition on age, the vaccine was 85–92% effective in every age group. The aggregate number was misleading because it ignored the partition — a phenomenon called **Simpson's Paradox**. The Law of Total Probability is the tool that prevents this error.

:::

### Partitions

:::{admonition} Definition 3.4: Partition
:class: important

A collection $\{B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_k\}$ is a **partition** of $S$ if:

1. $B_1 \cup B_2 \cup \cdots \cup B_k = S$ (the pieces cover everything), and
2. $B_i \cap B_j = \emptyset$ for all $i \neq j$ (the pieces don't overlap).

Every sample point belongs to exactly one $B_i$.

:::

The simplest partition is $\{B, B^c\}$. But partitions can have any number of pieces.

### The Law

:::{admonition} Theorem 3.4: The Law of Total Probability
:class: important

If $\{B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_k\}$ is a partition of $S$ with $P(B_i) > 0$ for all $i$, then for any event $A$:

$$P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P(A|B_i) \cdot P(B_i).$$

:::

:::{note}

**Proof.**

**Strategy:** Break $A$ into mutually exclusive pieces — one for each element of the partition — then apply Axiom 3 and the multiplication rule.

*Step 1.* Since $\{B_1, \ldots, B_k\}$ partitions $S$, every outcome in $A$ belongs to exactly one $B_i$. So we can write $A$ as the union of its pieces within each $B_i$:

$$A = A \cap S = A \cap (B_1 \cup B_2 \cup \cdots \cup B_k) = (A \cap B_1) \cup (A \cap B_2) \cup \cdots \cup (A \cap B_k).$$

*Step 2.* These pieces are mutually exclusive. Why? Because if $i \neq j$, then $B_i \cap B_j = \emptyset$ (the partition pieces don't overlap), so:

$$(A \cap B_i) \cap (A \cap B_j) = A \cap (B_i \cap B_j) = A \cap \emptyset = \emptyset.$$

*Step 3.* Since the pieces are mutually exclusive, Axiom 3 lets us add their probabilities:

$$P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P(A \cap B_i).$$

*Step 4.* Now apply the multiplication rule (Theorem 3.3) to each term: $P(A \cap B_i) = P(A|B_i) \cdot P(B_i)$.

$$P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P(A|B_i) \cdot P(B_i). \quad \blacksquare$$

**Why this matters:** The total probability of $A$ is a *weighted* average of $P(A|B_i)$, with weights $P(B_i)$. The larger a partition piece, the more it contributes. This is why a simple average of conditional probabilities is wrong when the pieces have unequal sizes — and why Simpson's Paradox occurs.

:::

:::{admonition} Calculus Flashback: Summation Notation
:class: important

The Law of Total Probability uses $\sum_{i=1}^{k}$, which means "add up the terms for $i = 1, 2, \ldots, k$":

$$\sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i = a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_k.$$

This is the same sigma notation you saw in calculus for Riemann sums: $\sum_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i) \Delta x$. Here, each term $P(A|B_i) \cdot P(B_i)$ plays the role of $f(x_i) \cdot \Delta x$ — a value times a weight. In later chapters, when we move to continuous distributions, this sum will become an integral: $P(A) = \int P(A|x) f(x)\, dx$. The Law of Total Probability is the discrete version of this integral.

:::

### Simpson's Paradox: When Aggregation Lies

**EXAMPLE 3.13: The Kidney Stone Paradox (Real Data)**

This is a real medical study (Charig et al., *BMJ*, 1986). Two kidney stone treatments were compared:

| | Treatment A (open surgery) | Treatment B (percutaneous) |
|:---|:---:|:---:|
| **Overall success rate** | **78%** (273/350) | **83%** (289/350) |

Treatment B looks better overall. But now stratify by stone size:

| Stone Size | Treatment A | Treatment B |
|:---|:---:|:---:|
| Small stones | **93%** (81/87) | 87% (234/270) |
| Large stones | **73%** (192/263) | 69% (55/80) |

**Treatment A wins in BOTH groups** — but loses overall!

How is this possible? Doctors assigned the harder cases (large stones) disproportionately to Treatment A. Since large stones have lower success rates regardless of treatment, Treatment A's aggregate was dragged down by having more difficult cases.

This is **Simpson's Paradox**: a trend that appears in aggregated data *reverses* when the data are stratified by a confounding variable.

**The mathematical explanation** is the Law of Total Probability with unequal weights:

- Treatment A: $P(\text{success}) = (0.93)(87/350) + (0.73)(263/350) = 0.231 + 0.548 = 0.780.$
- Treatment B: $P(\text{success}) = (0.87)(270/350) + (0.69)(80/350) = 0.671 + 0.158 = 0.829.$

Treatment B has more weight on the easy group (small stones: 270/350 = 77%) while Treatment A has more weight on the hard group (large stones: 263/350 = 75%). The *weights* — not the conditional success rates — drive the aggregate reversal.

**The lesson:** Never compare aggregate rates without asking "what are the rates *conditional on* relevant subgroups?" Simpson's Paradox is the Law of Total Probability warning you that the weighting scheme matters.

:class: note

The **Law of Total Probability** is a "divide and conquer" strategy: split the sample space into pieces where conditional probabilities are known, then combine with a weighted sum.

**Simpson's Paradox** is what happens when you forget the weights. A trend can reverse when data are aggregated across groups with different compositions. The cure: always condition on relevant subgroups before comparing.

**Connection forward:** The Law of Total Probability gives us the denominator for Bayes' Rule (next section). These two results are inseparable partners.

## Bayes' Rule

:::{admonition} What Are We Going to Learn?
:class: note

**The big idea:** The Law of Total Probability computes $P(A)$ from conditional probabilities $P(A|B_i)$. Bayes' Rule goes the *other direction*: given that $A$ has occurred, which $B_i$ was responsible? It "inverts" conditional probability — turning $P(\text{data} | \text{cause})$ into $P(\text{cause} | \text{data})$.

**Why this matters right now:** You take a medical test. It's positive. The test is "99% accurate." You think: "I'm 99% likely to be sick." *You're almost certainly wrong.* And if you're a doctor who makes this mistake — as studies show the majority do — you may subject a healthy patient to invasive, risky follow-up procedures based on a false alarm. Bayes' Rule tells you the truth, and the truth depends on something most people forget: *how common the disease is in the first place*.

:::

### A Brief History: The Theorem That Almost Died

:::{note}

**Thomas Bayes (c. 1701–1761)** was a Presbyterian minister in Tunbridge Wells, England. He published just two works in his lifetime — neither on probability. The essay that bears his name, *"An Essay towards solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances,"* was found among his papers after his death by his friend **Richard Price**, who edited it, added an introduction, and communicated it to the Royal Society. It was published in the *Philosophical Transactions* in 1764 — three years after Bayes died.

The French mathematician **Pierre-Simon Laplace** independently rediscovered and generalized the result in 1774, putting it in the form we use today.

For most of the 20th century, the theorem was controversial. The dominant school of statistics (led by R. A. Fisher and Jerzy Neyman) rejected it on philosophical grounds. But Bayesian methods quietly proved their worth: Alan Turing used Bayesian reasoning at Bletchley Park to break the Nazi Enigma code during World War II, and the U.S. Navy used Bayesian search theory to locate a lost hydrogen bomb off the coast of Spain in 1966 and the submarine USS *Scorpion* in 1968.

Today, Bayes' Rule is everywhere: spam filters, medical diagnostics, machine learning, autonomous vehicles, weather prediction, and the legal system.

:::

### Motivating Example: The Rare Disease Test

A disease affects 1 in 1,000 people. A test has 99% sensitivity (true positive rate) and 95% specificity (5% false positive rate). You test positive. $P(\text{you have the disease})$?

Most people — including most physicians (a study by David Eddy found 95 out of 100 got this wrong) — guess "about 99%." Let's compute the actual answer.

Let $D$ = "has disease," $+$ = "tests positive."

- $P(D) = 0.001$, $P(D^c) = 0.999$.
- $P(+ | D) = 0.99$ (sensitivity).
- $P(+ | D^c) = 0.05$ (false positive rate).

First, $P(+)$ by the Law of Total Probability:

$$P(+) = P(+|D) P(D) + P(+|D^c) P(D^c) = (0.99)(0.001) + (0.05)(0.999) = 0.00099 + 0.04995 = 0.05094.$$

Now:

$$P(D | +) = \frac{P(+ | D) \cdot P(D)}{P(+)} = \frac{(0.99)(0.001)}{0.05094} = \frac{0.00099}{0.05094} \approx 0.0194.$$

**The probability you have the disease is about 1.9% — NOT 99%.**

### Understanding the Surprise: Natural Frequency Reframing

The result above shocks most people. The psychologist Gerd Gigerenzer showed that reframing the problem using **natural frequencies** (counts instead of probabilities) dramatically improves comprehension. Let's try it:

**Imagine 100,000 people from the general population get tested.**

- **100 have the disease** (prevalence = 0.001).
- Of these 100, **99 test positive** (sensitivity = 99%).
- **99,900 do NOT have the disease.**
- Of these 99,900, **4,995 test positive** (false positive rate = 5%).

**Total positive tests:** $99 + 4{,}995 = 5{,}094$.

**Of those 5,094 positives, only 99 actually have the disease:** $99/5{,}094 \approx 1.9\%$.

The 99 true positives are drowning in a sea of 4,995 false alarms. The "99% accurate" test produces a positive result that's a false alarm 98% of the time — because the disease is rare.

**This is why base rates matter.** A test can be excellent at detecting a disease but terrible as a *screening* tool when the disease is uncommon. This is also why a positive screening test always needs confirmation with a second, more specific test.

### The Formal Statement

:::{admonition} Theorem 3.5: Bayes' Rule
:class: important

If $\{B_1, B_2, \ldots, B_k\}$ is a partition of $S$ with $P(B_i) > 0$ for all $i$, and $P(A) > 0$, then:

$$P(B_j | A) = \frac{P(A|B_j) \cdot P(B_j)}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k} P(A|B_i) \cdot P(B_i)}.$$

:::

:::{note}

**Proof.**

**Strategy:** Combine the definition of conditional probability (Definition 3.1) with the Law of Total Probability (Theorem 3.4). This proof is just two substitutions — but understanding *why* each substitution works is the key.

*Step 1.* Start with the definition of conditional probability applied to $P(B_j | A)$:

$$P(B_j | A) = \frac{P(A \cap B_j)}{P(A)}.$$

*This says: the probability that $B_j$ caused $A$ equals the probability that $A$ and $B_j$ both occur, divided by the total probability of $A$. We know both pieces.*

*Step 2.* The numerator: apply the multiplication rule (Theorem 3.3) to $P(A \cap B_j)$:

$$P(A \cap B_j) = P(A|B_j) \cdot P(B_j).$$

*This converts "A and $B_j$ both happen" into "the probability of $A$ given $B_j$, times the probability of $B_j$" — which are quantities we typically know.*

*Step 3.* The denominator: apply the Law of Total Probability (Theorem 3.4) to $P(A)$:

$$P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P(A|B_i) \cdot P(B_i).$$

*This computes the total probability of $A$ across all possible causes — the normalizing constant that ensures our posteriors sum to 1.*

*Step 4.* Substitute Steps 2 and 3 into Step 1:

$$P(B_j | A) = \frac{P(A|B_j) \cdot P(B_j)}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k} P(A|B_i) \cdot P(B_i)}. \quad \blacksquare$$

:::

### The Vocabulary of Bayes' Rule

| Term | Symbol | Meaning |
|:---|:---:|:---|
| **Prior** | $P(B_j)$ | What you believed about $B_j$ *before* observing evidence |
| **Likelihood** | $P(A|B_j)$ | How probable the evidence is, assuming $B_j$ is true |
| **Evidence** | $P(A)$ | Total probability of the evidence (the denominator) |
| **Posterior** | $P(B_j|A)$ | Your *updated* belief about $B_j$ after observing $A$ |

In words: **posterior = (likelihood × prior) / evidence.**

Bayes' Rule is a *learning machine*. You start with a prior belief, observe data, and update.

### Worked Examples

**EXAMPLE 3.14: Resolving the Prosecutor's Fallacy**

Return to Sally Clark. The prosecution presented $P(\text{two SIDS deaths} | \text{innocent}) = 1/73{,}000{,}000$. But the jury needed $P(\text{innocent} | \text{two deaths})$.

Bayes' Rule:

$$P(\text{innocent} | \text{evidence}) = \frac{P(\text{evidence} | \text{innocent}) \cdot P(\text{innocent})}{P(\text{evidence})}.$$

The prosecution compared $P(\text{evidence} | \text{innocent})$ to $P(\text{evidence} | \text{guilty})$ but ignored the **prior**: double infanticide is *also* extremely rare. The statistician Ray Hill later showed that the likelihood ratio $P(\text{two SIDS})/P(\text{two murders})$ actually **favored SIDS by 4.5:1 to 9:1**. The "1 in 73 million" number was not only wrong (it assumed independence) but also irrelevant without the Bayesian comparison.

The Prosecutor's Fallacy is precisely the error of presenting $P(\text{evidence}|\text{cause})$ as if it were $P(\text{cause}|\text{evidence})$. Bayes' Rule is the only correct way to invert the conditional.

**EXAMPLE 3.15: Identifying the Source of a Defective Part**

Three suppliers:

| Supplier | Market Share | Defect Rate |
|:---|:---:|:---:|
| Alpha | 50% | 1% |
| Beta | 30% | 3% |
| Gamma | 20% | 5% |

A defective component is found. Which supplier most likely produced it?

**Step 1:** $P(D) = (0.01)(0.50) + (0.03)(0.30) + (0.05)(0.20) = 0.005 + 0.009 + 0.010 = 0.024.$

**Step 2:** Bayes' Rule for each:

$$P(\text{Alpha}|D) = \frac{(0.01)(0.50)}{0.024} = \frac{0.005}{0.024} \approx 0.208$$

$$P(\text{Beta}|D) = \frac{(0.03)(0.30)}{0.024} = \frac{0.009}{0.024} = 0.375$$

$$P(\text{Gamma}|D) = \frac{(0.05)(0.20)}{0.024} = \frac{0.010}{0.024} \approx 0.417$$

**Gamma is most likely** (41.7%) even though it provides only 20% of parts — because it has the highest defect rate. Bayes' Rule shifts blame toward the supplier with the worst quality.

Check: $0.208 + 0.375 + 0.417 = 1.000.$ $\checkmark$

**IN R**

```r
# Bayes' Rule: Supplier identification
suppliers <- c("Alpha", "Beta", "Gamma")
p_supplier <- c(0.50, 0.30, 0.20)
p_defect_given <- c(0.01, 0.03, 0.05)

p_defect <- sum(p_defect_given * p_supplier)
posterior <- (p_defect_given * p_supplier) / p_defect

for (i in 1:3) cat("P(", suppliers[i], "| defect) =", round(posterior[i], 4), "\n")
cat("Most likely source:", suppliers[which.max(posterior)], "\n")
```

**EXAMPLE 3.16: The Spam Filter Revisited**

From Example 3.12, $P(\text{marked as spam}) = 0.398$. If an email is marked as spam, is it actually spam?

$$P(\text{spam} | \text{marked}) = \frac{P(\text{marked} | \text{spam}) \cdot P(\text{spam})}{P(\text{marked})} = \frac{(0.95)(0.40)}{0.398} = \frac{0.38}{0.398} \approx 0.955.$$

A flagged email has a 95.5% chance of actually being spam. The remaining 4.5% are innocent emails caught in the net.

*Historical note:* In August 2002, programmer Paul Graham published *"A Plan for Spam,"* describing a Bayesian spam filter that computed $P(\text{spam} | \text{words in email})$ using Bayes' Rule with word frequencies as likelihoods. His approach — essentially the Law of Total Probability applied token by token — achieved over 99.5% accuracy and became the basis for SpamAssassin, Mozilla Thunderbird's filter, and most modern spam detection.

### Why the Base Rate Matters: A Simulation

```r
# Rare disease simulation: see Bayes' Rule in action
set.seed(789)
n <- 100000; prevalence <- 0.001; sensitivity <- 0.99; fpr <- 0.05

has_disease <- rbinom(n, 1, prevalence)
test_positive <- ifelse(has_disease, rbinom(n, 1, sensitivity), rbinom(n, 1, fpr))

cat("People with disease:", sum(has_disease), "\n")
cat("Total positive tests:", sum(test_positive), "\n")
cat("  True positives:", sum(test_positive & has_disease), "\n")
cat("  False positives:", sum(test_positive & !has_disease), "\n")
cat("\nSimulated P(disease|+) =", round(sum(test_positive & has_disease)/sum(test_positive), 4), "\n")
cat("Theoretical P(disease|+) =", round(0.00099/0.05094, 4), "\n")
```

:class: tip

(a) Overall pass rate?
(b) A chip passes. $P(\text{from Process A})$?
(c) A chip fails. $P(\text{from Process B})$?

:::{admonition} Solution
:class: tip

(a) $P(\text{pass}) = (0.95)(0.70) + (0.80)(0.30) = 0.665 + 0.24 = 0.905.$

(b) $P(A|\text{pass}) = (0.95 \times 0.70)/0.905 = 0.665/0.905 \approx 0.735.$

(c) $P(B|\text{fail}) = P(\text{fail}|B) \cdot P(B) / P(\text{fail}) = (0.20)(0.30)/(0.095) = 0.06/0.095 \approx 0.632.$

:::

::::

:::{admonition} What Did We Learn?
:class: note

**Bayes' Rule inverts conditional probability.** Given evidence, it tells you the probability of the cause. The formula has three ingredients: a prior, a likelihood, and a normalizing constant from the Law of Total Probability.

**The base rate matters enormously.** A "99% accurate" test applied to a rare disease mostly produces false alarms. The natural frequency reframing (think in counts, not probabilities) makes this clear: 99 true positives vs. 4,995 false positives.

**The Prosecutor's Fallacy resolved:** Confusing $P(\text{evidence}|\text{cause})$ with $P(\text{cause}|\text{evidence})$ is the single most dangerous probability error. Bayes' Rule is the antidote — and it requires you to account for the base rate.

## The Event-Composition Method

:::{admonition} What Are We Going to Learn?
:class: note

**The big idea:** Instead of listing every sample point, you express the event of interest as a *composition* of simpler events using $\cup$, $\cap$, and complements — then apply the laws of probability. This is how engineers compute the reliability of real systems with multiple components.

**Why this matters right now:** The Space Shuttle *Challenger* had two O-ring seals per joint — primary and secondary — designed as redundant protection. Under an independence assumption, $P(\text{both fail}) = P(\text{primary fails}) \times P(\text{secondary fails})$ — vanishingly small. But at 31°F on launch morning, a phenomenon called "joint rotation" could lift *both* seals simultaneously. The backup wasn't truly independent of the primary. The event composition was wrong, and seven people died.

:::

### The Strategy

1. **Define the experiment** and identify the target event.
2. **Identify the component events** you can work with.
3. **Express the target** as a composition using $\cup$, $\cap$, complements.
4. **Apply probability laws** (additive, multiplicative, complement) to compute the answer.

The hardest step is (3). Always verify your composition contains the *same* sample points as the target event.

### Worked Examples

**EXAMPLE 3.17: Parallel and Series System**

A fire suppression system: two sprinklers ($A$, $B$) in parallel (at least one must work) and an alarm ($C$) in series (must work). Reliabilities: $P(A) = 0.95$, $P(B) = 0.90$, $P(C) = 0.99$. All independent.

System works $= C \cap (A \cup B)$.

$$P(A \cup B) = 1 - P(A^c)P(B^c) = 1 - (0.05)(0.10) = 0.995.$$

$$P(\text{system}) = P(C) \cdot P(A \cup B) = (0.99)(0.995) = 0.98505.$$

About 98.5% — better than any individual sprinkler, thanks to the parallel design.

**EXAMPLE 3.18: "At Least One" via Complement**

Five friends each independently attempt a puzzle with success probabilities 0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7.

$$P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - P(\text{none}) = 1 - (0.7)(0.6)(0.5)(0.4)(0.3) = 1 - 0.0252 = 0.9748.$$

The complement method turned a five-event union into a simple product. This is the most powerful trick in event composition.

:::{admonition} Calculus Flashback: The Geometric Series
:class: important

You'll need this repeatedly in Chapters 4–7. If $|r| < 1$:

$$\sum_{i=0}^{\infty} r^i = \frac{1}{1-r}, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} r^i = \frac{r}{1-r}, \qquad \sum_{i=0}^{n} r^i = \frac{1-r^{n+1}}{1-r}.$$

**Example:** The probability that the first success in repeated independent trials occurs on trial $k$ is $(1-p)^{k-1}p$ (we'll call this the Geometric distribution in Chapter 4). The total probability sums to 1:

$$\sum_{k=1}^{\infty} (1-p)^{k-1} p = p \cdot \frac{1}{1-(1-p)} = p \cdot \frac{1}{p} = 1. \quad \checkmark$$

Also recall: $e^x = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} x^k / k!$. This will be essential for the Poisson distribution (Chapter 4).

:::

:::{admonition} What Did We Learn?
:class: note

The **event-composition method** is a strategy: express complex events as compositions of simpler ones, then apply the tools from this chapter.

**Two power moves:** (1) "At least one" = $1 - P(\text{none})$. (2) Parallel systems use $P(A \cup B) = 1 - P(A^c)P(B^c)$; series systems use $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$.

**The Challenger lesson:** Redundancy only works when failures are *truly independent*. Common-cause failures defeat redundancy.

:::

## Random Variables: A First Look

:::{admonition} What Are We Going to Learn?
:class: note

**The big idea:** Everything in Chapters 2–3 is about *events*. But in practice, we care about *numbers*: How many defective items? How long until the next bus? What's the stock price? A **random variable** is a function that assigns a number to each outcome — the bridge from events to the quantitative probability of Chapters 4–7.

:::

### From Events to Numbers

Flip a coin three times. The sample space is $S = \{HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT\}$.

Now define $X$ = number of heads. $X$ is a function from $S$ to $\{0, 1, 2, 3\}$:

| Outcome | $X$ | | Outcome | $X$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| HHH | 3 | | THH | 2 |
| HHT | 2 | | THT | 1 |
| HTH | 2 | | TTH | 1 |
| HTT | 1 | | TTT | 0 |

We can now ask: $P(X = 2) = 3/8$ (three outcomes give exactly 2 heads).

:::{admonition} Definition 3.5: Random Variable (Informal)
:class: important

A **random variable** is a numerical function defined on a sample space. It assigns a real number to each outcome.

**Conventions:**

- Random variables: UPPERCASE ($X$, $Y$, $Z$)
- Observed values: lowercase ($x$, $y$, $z$)
- "$P(X = 2)$" means "the probability that the random variable $X$ takes the value 2"

:::

:::{admonition} Common Mistake: The Random Variable Is Not a Number
:class: warning

A random variable $X$ is a *function* — a rule that maps outcomes to numbers. A specific value like $x = 2$ is a *realization* — the number you get for a particular outcome. Saying "$X$ is a Binomial" means $X$ follows a Binomial *distribution* (a probability rule). It does NOT mean $X$ equals a particular number.

Think of it this way: $X$ is the *recipe*; $x$ is the *meal*. The same recipe can produce different meals on different occasions.

:::

**IN R**

```r
# Random variable: number of heads in 3 flips
set.seed(321); n_sim <- 100000
x <- rbinom(n_sim, size = 3, prob = 0.5)
cat("Simulated distribution of X:\n"); print(round(table(x)/n_sim, 4))
cat("\nTheoretical: P(X=0)=1/8, P(X=1)=3/8, P(X=2)=3/8, P(X=3)=1/8\n")
```

:::{admonition} What Did We Learn?
:class: note

A **random variable** converts qualitative outcomes into quantitative values. This lets us use calculus, expectation, and variance. Starting in Chapter 4, we shift from "what is the probability of this *event*?" to "what is the probability that this *number* takes a specific value?" Everything from Chapter 3 — conditional probability, independence, Bayes' Rule — carries forward directly.

:::

---

:::{admonition} Common Mistakes — Chapter 3
:class: warning

1. **Confusing $P(A|B)$ with $P(B|A)$.** The most dangerous error in probability. Always identify what's *given* (after the bar) and what you're *computing* (before the bar).

2. **Confusing independence with mutually exclusive.** Mutually exclusive = *cannot* happen together ($P(A \cap B) = 0$). Independent = *don't influence* each other ($P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$). If both have positive probability, they cannot be both.

3. **Averaging conditional probabilities without weighting.** $P(A)$ is a *weighted* average of $P(A|B_i)$, not a simple average. This error produces Simpson's Paradox.

4. **Multiplying without checking independence.** $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$ *only* when independent. For dependent events, use $P(A)P(B|A)$.

5. **Ignoring the base rate.** A "99% accurate" test applied to a rare disease mostly produces false alarms. Always use Bayes' Rule with the prior probability.

6. **Assuming independence without justification.** The 2008 financial crisis and the Fukushima disaster both resulted from this error. Always ask: *what shared cause could make these events fail together?*

:::

## Chapter Summary

1. **Conditional probability** $P(A|B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)$ measures $P(A)$ in the restricted universe where $B$ occurred.

2. **$P(A|B) \neq P(B|A)$ in general.** Confusing these is the Prosecutor's Fallacy.

3. **Independence** means $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$. Equivalent: $P(A|B) = P(A)$.

4. **Independence ≠ mutually exclusive.** These are nearly opposite when both events have positive probability.

5. **The multiplication rule:** $P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B|A)$. Extends to chains. Visualized via tree diagrams.

6. **The Law of Total Probability:** $P(A) = \sum P(A|B_i) P(B_i)$ — a weighted average over a partition. Simpson's Paradox is what happens when you forget the weights.

7. **Bayes' Rule:** $P(B_j|A) = P(A|B_j)P(B_j)/P(A)$. Updates prior beliefs with evidence. Base rates matter enormously.

8. **Event-composition method:** Express complex events using $\cup$, $\cap$, complements. The complement method ($1 - P(\text{none})$) is especially powerful.

9. **A random variable** is a numerical function on the sample space — the bridge to Chapters 4–7.

:::{admonition} Think About It
:class: tip

You've learned the most misunderstood concept in probability: the difference between $P(A|B)$ and $P(B|A)$. You've seen how confusing them sent an innocent woman to prison.

Here's the uncomfortable question: **how many of your own daily judgments confuse cause and evidence?**

When you see a successful person and assume they must be talented, you're estimating $P(\text{talented} | \text{successful})$. But what you've actually observed is $P(\text{successful} | \text{talented})$ — a very different number, because luck, timing, and privilege all play enormous roles.

When a news headline says "people who eat chocolate live longer," your brain reads causation. But the data only says $P(\text{longevity} | \text{chocolate eater}) > P(\text{longevity})$. Maybe wealthier people eat more chocolate *and* have better healthcare. The conditional probability is real; the causal story might not be.

Bayes' Rule isn't just a formula. It's a discipline: *"What am I conditioning on? What am I computing? What's my prior?"* Develop this habit, and you will reason more clearly than almost anyone around you.

:::
