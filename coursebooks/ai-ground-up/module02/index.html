How Machine Learning Works

Module 2 · Without the Math

Content current as of April 2026. AI tools evolve rapidly — underlying principles remain applicable.

Learning Objectives

Builds on: Module 1 (What Is AI?)

After completing this module, you will be able to:

  1. Explain how machine learning differs from traditional programming — in one sentence
  2. Describe the three types of machine learning with real examples
  3. Walk through how a model learns: training, validation, and testing
  4. Explain what a neural network does without using a single equation
  5. Describe the data pipeline from collection to deployment
  6. Identify where bias enters machine learning systems — and why it’s hard to remove
  7. Critically evaluate AI outputs by understanding what the model learned from

The Algorithm That Learned to Be Sexist

In 2018, Reuters broke a story that stunned the tech world. Amazon had built a machine learning system to automate hiring — scan resumes, score candidates, surface the best ones. The model trained on ten years of Amazon’s hiring data to learn what a “good” resume looked like.

It worked beautifully. The scores were consistent. The system was fast. There was just one problem: the model had taught itself to penalize women.

Because Amazon’s tech workforce had been predominantly male for a decade, the model learned that “male” was a feature of successful candidates. It downgraded resumes that contained the word “women’s” — as in “women’s chess club” or “captain of the women’s soccer team.” It penalized graduates of two all-women’s colleges. It had learned, with mathematical precision, to replicate a decade of human bias.

Amazon scrapped the tool. But the lesson endures: machine learning doesn’t discover truth. It discovers patterns in data. If the data contains bias, the model learns bias — and serves it back to you with the authority of mathematics.

This module teaches you how machine learning actually works — not the equations, but the ideas.

The Big Idea: Learning from Data Instead of Following Rules

The One-Sentence Summary

Traditional programming: Humans write rules → computer follows them. Machine learning: Humans provide data → computer discovers the rules.

Imagine you want to teach someone to recognize spam emails.

The traditional approach: You write a list of rules. If the subject line contains “FREE MONEY,” it’s spam. If there are more than three exclamation marks, it’s spam. You write hundreds of rules, maintain the list forever. Spammers adapt, you write more rules.

The machine learning approach: You collect 100,000 emails — 50,000 spam, 50,000 not spam — and show them to the algorithm. You say: “Figure out what makes spam different from real email.” The algorithm examines every email, looking for patterns that distinguish the two categories. It discovers its own rules.

The second approach is how your email’s spam filter actually works. Nobody writes spam rules by hand anymore.

Common Misconceptions About Machine Learning
  • “ML is magic” — It’s math. Specifically, it’s optimization: adjusting numbers to minimize prediction errors. No magic, no consciousness, no understanding.
  • “More data always = better” — More representative data helps. More biased data just produces a more confidently biased model.
  • “ML is objective” — ML finds patterns in data. If the data reflects human decisions (hiring, lending, policing), the model inherits human biases — and scales them.
  • “The algorithm is neutral” — Choosing what to predict, what data to collect, and how to measure success are all human decisions that shape the outcome before the algorithm runs.
  • “ML will figure it out” — ML needs human oversight at every stage: data selection, feature engineering, model evaluation, deployment monitoring, and ethical review.

ML in YOUR Field — Real Examples

  • Healthcare
  • Business
  • Education
  • Agriculture
  • Supervised: Predicting hospital readmission risk from patient records (labeled: readmitted / not readmitted)
  • Unsupervised: Discovering patient subgroups with similar symptom patterns for personalized treatment
  • Reinforcement: Optimizing drug dosing schedules by learning from patient response data over time
  • Supervised: Predicting customer churn from purchase history (labeled: churned / retained)
  • Unsupervised: Segmenting customers by behavior to target marketing campaigns
  • Reinforcement: Dynamic pricing that adjusts in real-time based on demand signals
  • Supervised: Predicting which students are at risk of failing based on engagement data (labeled: passed / failed)
  • Unsupervised: Discovering common misconception patterns across thousands of student responses
  • Reinforcement: Adaptive learning systems that adjust difficulty based on student performance
  • Supervised: Classifying crop diseases from photos (labeled: healthy / diseased / specific disease type)
  • Unsupervised: Identifying zones within a field that have similar soil characteristics for variable-rate treatment
  • Reinforcement: Optimizing irrigation scheduling by learning from soil moisture sensors and weather data

The Three Flavors of Machine Learning

1. Supervised Learning: “Here’s the Answer Key”

You give the algorithm labeled examples — data with the correct answer — and it learns to predict answers for new data.

Real-world examples: Email spam detection (labeled “spam” or “not spam”), medical diagnosis (X-rays labeled “tumor” or “no tumor”), language translation (sentence pairs in two languages).

2. Unsupervised Learning: “Find the Patterns”

No labels. You give the algorithm raw data and say: “Find the structure.” It discovers groupings humans might not see.

Real-world examples: Customer segmentation, anomaly detection (finding cyberattacks), topic modeling across thousands of documents.

3. Reinforcement Learning: “Trial, Error, Reward”

The algorithm learns by doing — taking actions, receiving rewards or penalties, and adjusting strategy.

Real-world examples: AlphaGo beating the world Go champion, self-driving cars, TikTok’s algorithm learning what keeps you scrolling.

How a Model Learns: The Training Pipeline

Here’s the full process from raw data to deployed model:

RAW DATA → Clean & Label → Split (Train/Validate/Test)
                                    ↓
                            TRAINING LOOP:
                    Predict → Compare → Adjust → Repeat
                         (millions of iterations)
                                    ↓
                    Validate → Tune → Test on unseen data
                                    ↓
                    DEPLOY → Monitor for drift → Retrain

Step 1: Collect Data

Everything starts with data. This is where most problems begin. If data is biased, the model learns bias. Amazon’s hiring tool learned from biased data and produced biased outputs.

Step 2: Prepare and Split the Data

Data is cleaned and split into three sets:

  • Training set (~70-80%): What the model learns from
  • Validation set (~10-15%): Checks learning during training
  • Test set (~10-15%): Final evaluation on unseen data

Why split? Because memorization is not learning. A student who memorizes last year’s answer key fails when the professor writes new questions.

Step 3: Train the Model

The model processes training data in an optimization loop: predict → compare → adjust → repeat. Millions of times.

Step 4: Validate and Tune

If the model performs well on training data but poorly on validation data, it’s overfitting: memorizing rather than learning.

If it performs poorly on both, it’s underfitting: it hasn’t learned enough.

The Goldilocks Problem
  • Overfitting: Performs perfectly on training data, fails on new data. Learned the noise, not the signal.
  • Underfitting: Performs poorly on everything. Hasn’t captured the patterns.
  • Just right: Performs well on both known and unknown data. Learned the patterns, not the specific examples.

Concrete example: You’re building a model to predict house prices. It trains on 10,000 homes in one city. An overfit model memorizes that “houses on Elm Street sell for $400K” — it learned specific addresses, not the pattern (square footage + bedrooms + neighborhood quality → price). When tested on homes in a different city, it’s useless. An underfit model just predicts “every house costs $300K.” The right model learns that bigger homes in better neighborhoods cost more — a pattern that transfers to ANY city.

Step 5: Test and Evaluate

Final evaluation on data the model has never seen. The closest simulation of real-world performance.

Step 6: Deploy and Monitor

The model goes live — but real-world data changes over time (data drift). A model that worked in January might fail in June. A spam filter trained in 2023 may miss 2026’s spam tactics. Ongoing monitoring is essential.

✅ Test Yourself: The Training Process
  1. Why is data split into three sets? (Training = learn, Validation = check during training, Test = final evaluation on unseen data)
  2. What’s overfitting? (Memorizing training data rather than learning general patterns — performs well on training data, poorly on new data)
  3. What’s the #1 law of machine learning? (Garbage in, garbage out — the model is only as good as its data)
  4. A house price model that only works in the city it was trained on is an example of: (Overfitting — it memorized specific data instead of learning transferable patterns)

Neural Networks: A Simple Explanation

The Building Block: A Single Neuron

A neuron takes in numbers, multiplies each by a weight (how important that input is), adds them up, and passes the result through a function that decides whether to “fire.” One neuron is useless. Millions connected in layers learn extraordinarily complex patterns.

A concrete forward pass: Imagine a single neuron deciding if an email is spam. It receives three inputs: (1) number of exclamation marks = 5, (2) contains “FREE” = 1 (yes), (3) from known sender = 0 (no). Each input has a learned weight: exclamation marks × 0.3 = 1.5, contains FREE × 0.8 = 0.8, known sender × (-1.0) = 0.0. Sum = 2.3. If the threshold is 1.5, the neuron fires: spam. The weights were learned during training — adjusted millions of times until the neuron got good at catching spam without flagging real emails.

Layers: Input → Hidden → Output

  • Input layer: Receives raw data (pixels, words, numbers)
  • Hidden layers: Transform data, extracting increasingly abstract features. Early layers detect simple patterns (edges in images, common words in text). Deeper layers combine simple patterns into complex ones (edges → shapes → faces)
  • Output layer: Produces the result (“87% cat, 13% dog”)

Deep Learning = Many Hidden Layers

“Deep learning” just means many layers — sometimes dozens or hundreds. More layers = more complex patterns. Frontier LLMs are estimated to have dozens to over 100 layers, though most commercial model architectures (including GPT-4, Claude, and Gemini) are not publicly disclosed — these figures come from leaked estimates and inference, not official sources.

The analogy: A factory assembly line. Raw materials enter one end. Each station performs a transformation. No single station produces the finished product. But after dozens of stations, raw materials become a car. In a neural network, raw pixels enter the input layer. Each hidden layer transforms them. No single layer “understands” the image. But after many layers, the network correctly identifies a cat.

The Data Pipeline: Where Everything Begins

COLLECT → CLEAN → LABEL → TRAIN → EVALUATE → DEPLOY → MONITOR

Where Bias Enters the Pipeline

Stage How Bias Enters Example
Collection Data doesn’t represent the full population Medical AI trained mostly on white patients
Cleaning Removing “outliers” that are underrepresented groups Dropping non-English name applications
Labeling Human labelers bring their own biases Professional women labeled “receptionist”
Training Model optimizes for the majority pattern Amazon: “male” = “successful”
Evaluation Test set has the same biases as training set Model looks great — same blind spots
Deployment Applied to populations it wasn’t trained on US facial recognition deployed in Africa
The #1 Law of Machine Learning

Garbage in, garbage out. A model is only as good as its data. If the data reflects historical biases, the model reproduces them — often more efficiently and at greater scale than humans.

Guided Practice 2.3 — The Bias Spotter

A company builds an ML model to predict which job applicants will be “successful employees” based on 5 years of performance review data. Identify the bias risk at each stage:

  1. Data: What’s likely in 5 years of performance reviews? (Think: who does the reviewing?)
  2. Labels: How is “successful” defined? (Revenue generated? Manager ratings? Tenure?)
  3. Features: Which input variables might be proxies for protected characteristics?
  4. Evaluation: If the test set has the same biases as the training set, will you even detect the problem?
  5. Deployment: What happens when the model is applied to applicants from backgrounds underrepresented in the training data?
Key risks
  1. Data: Performance reviews carry well-documented biases — studies show women and minorities receive more subjective criticism and less credit for collaborative work.
  2. Labels: If “successful” means “high manager rating,” the model learns what managers reward — which may reflect bias, not performance. If it means “stayed 3+ years,” it penalizes people who were underpaid and left for better opportunities.
  3. Features: Zip code, university name, extracurricular activities, and even name formatting can be proxies for race, socioeconomic status, and national origin.
  4. Evaluation: Yes — this is the insidious part. If your test set has the same biases, the model looks great on paper while systematically discriminating.
  5. Deployment: The model will score these applicants as “low probability of success” because it has few examples of people like them succeeding — creating a self-fulfilling prophecy.
This is exactly what happened at Amazon (Module 2 hook). The model worked perfectly on biased data.

Evaluating AI Outputs: Five Questions to Always Ask

  1. What data was it trained on? (Who collected it? What’s missing?)
  2. What was it optimized for? (Accuracy? Engagement? Profit?)
  3. Who validated it? (Diverse populations? Diverse teams?)
  4. What are its known failure modes?
  5. Who benefits and who’s harmed?

Module 2 Summary

Key Takeaways
  1. Machine learning is learning from data, not following rules.
  2. Three types: Supervised, Unsupervised, Reinforcement.
  3. The training process splits data and iterates until the model generalizes well.
  4. Neural networks are layers of simple operations. Deep learning = many layers.
  5. Overfitting is memorization, not learning. The Goldilocks problem.
  6. Bias enters at every stage of the data pipeline.
  7. “Garbage in, garbage out” — the data shapes the model.
  8. Always ask: What data? What optimization? Who validated? Who benefits?
💭 Think About It

Machine learning doesn’t discover truth — it discovers patterns in data. And data is collected by humans, labeled by humans, and reflects human decisions about what to include and what to leave out. When an AI system discriminates, the tempting response is to blame the algorithm. But the algorithm did exactly what it was designed to do: find patterns in the data it was given. The real question isn’t “why is the AI biased?” The real question is “why is the data biased?” — and that’s a human question, not a technical one.
